⟦p011⟧This is, in its kind, the greatest, most prodigious and most marvellous deed that can be read of in any annals whatsoever, whether Greek or Latin, ancient or modern: in which one will indeed hear of several examples of certain persons so alike among themselves that those who saw them remained bewildered and confused, not knowing how to distinguish or recognise them, and often taking the one for the other; and that under the pretext of this re⟦p012⟧semblance, accompanied by a thousand frauds and lies, some men born in poor, low and humble station have known how to practise so well that they persuaded a whole people that they were sprung of a great, noble and illustrious race: as a Smerdis, an Archelaus, an Equitius, a Herophilus, and several others, whose history will be told below in a more convenient place. Nevertheless, if the circumstances are measured by a true ell and weighed in a just balance, this deed will appear incomparably more monstrous and astonishing than all the others: in none of which will it be found that such a likeness, though it was painted over and clothed in a thousand necessary lies, was so powerful as to impose upon all the kinsfolk, and even upon four sisters, and upon the uncle who had brought up the nephew from his childhood, as in this present case — nay (what must draw everyone into still greater wonder), upon the wife herself, who received another man for her husband, and with him conversed familiarly, as husband and wife do, for the space of three years and more, without ever perceiving, nor even suspecting, the fraud. Although in other substitutions women have often shown themselves sharper, quicker and more perspicacious in discovering and detecting them than men: as the history of the wife of Quintus Sertorius at Rome, and of Jeanne, daughter of Count Baldwin in Flanders, will well bear witness, as we shall relate more fully hereafter {a}. And touching the great likenesses that there have been between certain men, there have been several in the past, of which some were maintained without fraud, and others produced great and notable impostures. As to the first: once at Rome, Vibius and Publicius, persons of very low and base condition, so closely resembled the great Pompey that the Romans called them Pompeys, and sometimes named Pompey Vibius or Publicius {b}. A like resemblance was between Cornelius Scipio and a swineherd (or, according to others, a *victimarius*, that is to say, a dealer in beasts for sacrifice) who was called Serapion {c}. Likewise between Hybreas the Milesian, that great and renowned orator, and a slave whom history does not name — so much so that the ancients firmly believed that they were brothers {d}. To Mark Antony, in his ⟦p013⟧triumvirate, Toranius had sold two young boys as twins, because they were altogether alike, although the one was from France and the other from Asia. Which being discovered by Mark Antony, who had paid three hundred sesterces for them, coming to three thousand seven hundred and fifty *écus* of our money (for the sesterce, according to the reckoning of Budé and other learned persons, reduced to the money of France, was worth about twenty-five *écus*, which, multiplied three hundred times, comes, a little more or less, to the said sum of 3,750 *écus*), he was at first sight a little vexed by it; but Toranius showed him that the thing he complained of ought to be esteemed by him the most precious part of his purchase. For if the children had been twins, there would have been nothing marvellous in their being alike, since they would have been begotten of one and the same seed, under the same stars and constellations; but to see two children born of different parents, in different countries, and so far apart, the one in Asia, the other in Europe, was a prodigious and greatly astonishing thing. With which answer Mark Antony was so well content that he was wont to say that in all the greatness of his possessions he had nothing so dear nor so precious as these two boys {e}. Once in Sicily there was a fisherman so like Sura, a Roman, at that time proconsul there, that they were alike not only in bodily resemblance but also in manner of speech, for both were stammerers. To whom Sura once said in jest that he greatly marvelled how he came to be so like him, seeing that my father (said Sura) was never in this country — meaning thereby to tax the honour and chastity of the poor fisherman's mother; who nonetheless did not show himself a dolt in answering him, saying that Sura had no cause to marvel at it, for his own father had often been at Rome: throwing back by this means upon Sura's mother what Sura had wished to stamp upon his own {f}. Sebastian Münster, a man of great reading, relates in his Cosmography that after the third defeat of the Burgundians by the Swiss (in which their Duke Charles was killed), which was about the year one thousand four hundred and seventy-seven, there came a man to Bruxelles, a town of the diocese of Speyer, who so naturally resembled the late ⟦p014⟧Duke Charles that the people constantly affirmed that the Duke was not dead, and that this man was truly Duke Charles, although he himself affirmed the contrary and stoutly denied that he was {g}. Francesco Sforza, Duke of Milan, had in his service a young soldier who resembled him so well that all the other soldiers, taking their cue from this likeness, often called the young man the Prince; in whom, as in a mirror, the Duke often delighted to see his own image, his gestures and bearing, to recognise his voice, and to contemplate himself. At the same time and in the same country this Duke Sforza had a jester named Marchesino, whom he called the lord Sigismondo Malatesta, his son, because they were entirely alike; at which this Malatesta was so much ashamed that when he wished to go to Milan to see the Duke, his father-in-law, he first sent to beg him to send Marchesino elsewhere. {h} Of the likenesses that have been the cause of several impostures, great factions and memorable enterprises, we shall speak more conveniently below in some place, if it please the Lord. But here perhaps someone will wish to search out and understand the cause why one often sees men begotten of different parents, and in different and distant places, nevertheless resemble one another so well and so exactly in the features of the face and the composition of the body that they cannot easily be distinguished from one another. To whom I shall say first that one must, with the honour and reverence that are due, refer the source and cause of such facts to the understanding of that great workman of nature (who is God almighty), who is not bound to races, nor to countries, nor to the affections of persons, but by his infinite and incomprehensible providence, and his secret, high and inscrutable judgments, projects his ideas and forms his creatures as seems good to him. Nevertheless one sees it come about most often that the likenesses of men pass down through their races, and that every animal not only begets its like, but also imparts to it its own proper and natural virtues.

⟦p015⟧The brave are begotten of the brave and good; there is in bullocks, there is in horses, the virtue of their sires; nor do fierce eagles beget the unwarlike dove {k}.

So far as to see posterity bear now the sinews, now the scars, now whatever other marks of its ancestors and of its origin: witness the Roman Lepidi, of whom there were three of one house each having an eye covered with a little membrane; and Nicaeus of Byzantium, who was born black as a Moor, taking after his grandfather rather than his mother, who was fair and white, though begotten in adultery by an Ethiopian {l}. But the forces of nature and of race are not so great but that we sometimes see posterity degenerate and become unlike its progenitors: as from the handsome are born the ugly and deformed; from the robust and strong issue the impotent and weak; from the good and virtuous proceed the vicious and wicked {m}. Otherwise, if this did not sometimes happen so, it would be necessary, for the public good, to forbid by a general and inviolable law to the ugly, the feeble and the wicked marriage and carnal company with women, so that all might infallibly be born handsome, powerful and robust. Whereupon Alexander of Aphrodisias labours hard to sound out and show the cause why one often sees it happen that a stupid, gross and foolish man, nay a simpleton, will produce children who are clever, prudent, wise and discreet. And he concludes the reason to be that a dolt, in the venereal act, lets himself be so overcome and vanquished by the present pleasure that he then thinks of nothing else, having willingly plunged his mind and soul into the body; whence the seed, being drawn and taken from that body in which the mind is found, partakes greatly of the reasonable virtue, and makes the children who descend from it more prudent and intelligent than the father. As, on the contrary, with those who are ingenious, discreet or learned: because their mind incessantly works and busies itself elsewhere than with the pleasure of the flesh — even at the very instant of the act, in which it does not at all let itself be van⟦p016⟧quished — it comes about that the seed which afterwards flows forth, having nothing but of the body (for the mind was wandering elsewhere), has likewise not much reasonable and natural virtue {n}: which was (in the judgment of many) the cause why Aristarchus of Alexandria, a man of singular and commendable erudition, nevertheless begot Aristagoras and Aristarchus, his children, dull, stolid and almost simpletons {o}. On this subject the reader will take it in good part if I transcribe the words of Spartianus, who, writing to the Emperor Diocletian: It is certain (said he), Augustus, that there has been almost none of those great and illustrious personages who have left children good and useful to the commonwealth: for either they died without having any, or else had such that it would have been better for them beyond comparison never to have had any. And to begin with Romulus: he had no posterity. Numa Pompilius, his successor, had none who could profit the public. And then Camillus: had he children like himself? And Scipio, what? The Catos, what? — who were excellent and rare persons. But what shall I say of Homer, Demosthenes, Virgil, Sallust, Plautus, Terence? And what, moreover, of Caesar? And what of Cicero, for whom alone it would have been better to have had no children? What of Augustus, who did not even have a good adopted son, although he had the power to choose a good one out of a hundred thousand? Was not Trajan deceived in the choice he made of Hadrian his nephew? But let us come to natural sons. What greater good fortune could have come to Marcus Antoninus, Philosopher and Emperor, than if he had not left Commodus his heir? and to Septimius Severus, if he had not begotten Bassianus {p}? But to return to our track, and to search curiously into the cause of likenesses: I do not find amiss the opinion of Empedocles and of the natural philosophers, who think that this proceeds from the imagination which the woman may have conceived at the hour when she conceives, which has such power over the fruit that comes to be formed that the character of the image remains perpetually engraved upon it {q}. Whence in times past children have several times been seen born like the portraits which their mothers kept for their delight in their chambers and closets {r}: witness the woman who, having attentive⟦p017⟧ly gazed, at the instant she was conceiving, upon a painting of a Moor that hung about her bed, produced a child black as an Ethiopian. St Jerome somewhere relates that an ugly woman, married to a hideous and deformed husband, having given birth to a handsome boy, was on that account greatly suspected and accused of adultery, and nevertheless saved by the counsel and prudence of that sovereign physician Hippocrates, who caused inquiry to be made whether in the woman's chamber there was some beautiful painting like the child; which was found, and thus the woman was delivered from the crime and the suspicion {ſ}. The books of the philosophers are full of it, that the things seen by the woman at the point of conception have great virtue to give form and imprint characters upon the creature that is engendered. Wherein all married people who take pleasure in paintings will take instruction not to keep in their chambers any that are ugly, monstrous or deformed, in order to avoid such scandals. On which no one (as I believe) is ignorant of the history of Jacob [*nul ne ſçait*, Coras's idiom for *nul n'ignore*, as at annotation XVI], who, having agreed with Laban that every beast of the flocks speckled with any spots of diverse colour should be his for his wages, had green rods of diverse trees peeled and set in the watering-trough, together with the bark, so that the goats and ewes of the flock, looking upon the rods and the bark of differing colour, might likewise form their young speckled with unlike spots {t}. In the time of the Emperor Charles IV, some attest that a woman, for having gazed too fixedly, at the hour she was conceiving, upon an image of Saint John clothed in skins, gave birth to a daughter all hairy like a bear. Juan Luis Vives, a man well lettered and versed in all disciplines, relates that Margaret, daughter of the Emperor Maximilian, in his time told a story to Jean Lamuze, a learned man and ambassador of King Ferdinand of Hungary: that in a town of Brabant which he names Buscunducis, as a general procession was being made in honour of some saint, and according to their old ceremonies some were dressed in the form of angels and others in the habit of devils, one of the latter, bounding and leaping through the streets all in⟦p018⟧flamed, went straight to his house to find his wife, threw her upon the bed, telling her that he wished to get her with child of a devil. Which he did, or at least of a son who had the form of a little devil, and who began from the moment he was born to leap and bound {u}. And if the reader is not content, but further asks the reason why this impression of different forms, according to the conceptions, happens peculiarly to men more than to the other animals: I shall think I answer them sufficiently with Pliny, if I tell them that the quickness of thoughts, the celerity of the understanding and the diversity of minds imprint diverse forms and marks upon men, whereas in the other living souls the conceptions and thoughts are uniform and alike among all, and for each in its kind; and thus, not having this multiplicity of imaginations, forms and representations, they always bring forth their young resembling their sires and dams {x}. And because also persons, at the instant of the venereal pleasure, are most often occupied with nothing but the pleasure alone, in which they hold the mind without letting it stray to any other thought, it likewise comes about that children are commonly like their parents and progenitors. Whence some of our interpreters of the law determine an old question: if a woman, immediately after the death of her first husband, remarries, and at the end of nine months is delivered, to which of the two husbands the child ought to be adjudged {y}. For although many adjudge it to the first husband {z}, precisely because it is to be imputed to the second, who was in too great haste to marry the widow {a}; and others to the second, both because he has longer laboured and cultivated the ground {b}, that is to say, cohabited carnally with the woman, to the effect of this pregnancy, and also because the child was born in his house and during his marriage {c}; and a third party presume the child to belong to both, just as the law also sometimes takes the conjecture that a slave (who nevertheless can belong wholly to only one) belongs to two masters, and to each entirely {d}; and there are also some who, on account of the confusion and disturbance of the blood and of the seed {e}, presume it to be neither the first ⟦p019⟧nor the second husband's {f}: nevertheless some, for the reason I have stated, are of this heresy, that one ought prudently to consider which of the two husbands the child better resembles {g}, inasmuch as one commonly sees it happen — and Galen, that excellent physician, so demonstrates it — that children closely take after their fathers and parents {h}. I am not unaware, either, that many are in this error, of thinking that illegitimate children and bastards more resemble the putative father (who is the husband), because the woman, they say, during the act of lechery incessantly thinks of the husband, fearing his coming {i}; and the imaginations, as we have proved above, give form to the child who at that point is conceived and engendered {k}. Nevertheless everyone can easily judge, both by experience (mistress of all things) and by the authority of grave and learned persons, of the contrary, and that, as Phocylides the Greek poet said, beds defiled by lechery do not make children like the husbands.

*Non faciunt similes, stuprata cubilia natos.*

Whence Horace, praising the Emperor Augustus for having severely punished and repressed adultery, said among other things that by this means persons became more continent and chaste, and women made their posterity and lineage like the husband {l}.

The chaste house is polluted by no defilements; custom and law have tamed the spotted sin; mothers are praised for offspring like their sires; punishment follows hard upon guilt as its companion.

Moreover, we see that bastards resemble their true and natural father, not only in body and in the features and lineaments of the face, but also in manners and dispositions {m}. Besides that it is very little likely that at the point of pleasure, and in the instant of conception, the woman thinks more of the absent husband than of her lecher there present, whom she holds between her arms, and upon whom, with all her body and all her mind vehemently intent, she has her eyes incessantly fixed, ca⟦p020⟧ressing him by infinite lascivious and immodest means. And if one further seeks the cause why children sometimes resemble their fathers and at other times their mothers: I shall say with the Philosopher that if the virtue of the man is more abundant, the child will take after the father; and on the contrary, if the seed of the woman prevails, the child will take the form and likeness of the mother; and if there is an equal quantity of seed, it will resemble both, though in different places and parts of the body {n}. From which opinion Anaxagoras did not depart greatly when he said that the child will resemble that one of the parents who has contributed more seed.

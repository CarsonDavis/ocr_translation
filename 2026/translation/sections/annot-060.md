⟦p080⟧In the matter of proofs and of witnesses, it is certain and settled that singular witnesses prove nothing {a}, even though they were a hundred thousand in number {b}; for each deposes particularly to his own fact, and thus they hold the place of but one; and the deposition of one, of whatever dignity, greatness or authority he may be, is counted for nothing {c}: which Cicero in a certain place discourses upon and shows elegantly {d}. It is quite true that in certain cases singular witnesses (according to the interpretation of several) may [prove] sufficiently: as if it is a question of proving infamy, madness, heresy, or an act in general {e}; or else of proving an act to whose essence neither place nor time is required, such as a jurisdiction, a choice of burial, rumour and repute {f}. Whence several have, I know not how, thought that to convict a man of heresy two witnesses suffice, even though one of them should depose to one kind of heresy [and the other to another]: heresies, they say, although they are designated by divers names, are nevertheless interlinked and conjoined in wickedness {h}. But such an opinion could not be sustained, nor founded upon any text of law, although some (says Panormitanus), by a light and too inconsiderate interpretation, have written to the contrary {i}. And still less is it true that, to prove a universal or general act, singular witnesses suffice {k}: for custom in itself is a universal act, regarding all those ⟦p081⟧of a city, of a country or of a province {l}, and nevertheless, to make it appear, it does not suffice that the witnesses depose separately to divers acts from which the custom may be introduced, unless there are two at the least who have together seen each of the said acts {m}. The contrary opinion, then, is the sounder, and justly received by the sovereign courts, which will not judge rashly, but compass and measure all things by a straight ell, and weigh them in a just balance: especially in crimes, which, the greater and more horrible they are, the more must they be treated and determined with the greater circumspection and prudence {n}. And therefore one must conclude that in any crime whatsoever, however enormous it be, singular witnesses prove nothing; but it is necessary, to have certain and conclusive proof, that two (at the least) depose particularly to one and the same act {o}. Nay, by the law, several singular witnesses would not suffice to condemn a man of heresy, even though he were defamed of it {p}. And in truth, inasmuch as this crime is the most grievous, and above all execrable, directly regarding the divine majesty, so much the more must the Judges look to it the more closely, that it be not judged by opinion and lightly, but rightly and in truth {q}; in order also to obviate the strange and prodigious conspiracies, calumnies and vengeance that many wicked men daily practise against good people under the cloak and pretext of religion {r}, with which they mask themselves, to cover their dissolutions, lecheries, thefts, extortions, and a thousand other kinds of wickedness and wretched impieties. Returning, then, to the matter from which we have strayed, it would not be reasonable that the depositions of witnesses who could not have been together at the fact to which they depose should be received as [those] of several {ſ}.

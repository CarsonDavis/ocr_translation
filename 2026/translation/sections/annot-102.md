⟦p144⟧Picardy is a part of Belgic Gaul. Whereupon it must be understood that Gaul was formerly divided principally in two: namely into the Transalpine and the Cisalpine. The Cisalpine was called by the Romans that which took its beginning at the foot of the Alps and extended as far as the river Rubicon, the ancient boundary of Italy, which comes down from the Apennines and, passing by Rimini and Ravenna, enters the Adriatic sea. The Transalpine ⟦p145⟧was the name of that which is on this side of the Alps — which to us is Cisalpine — and it was divided by Julius Caesar and the others into three: namely into the Belgic, the Celtic and the Aquitanian. The Celts are separated from the Aquitanians by the river Garonne, descending from the mountains of Comminges, and from the Belgians by the rivers Marne (so called from Marneuf, a village one league from Langres, whence it comes) and Seine, coming from the Duchy of Burgundy. The Belgians are separated from the Germans, whom we call *Allemans*, by the river Rhine. In Belgic Gaul are the provinces of Flanders, Lorraine, Picardy and Normandy. Picardy takes its source at the borders of the Duchy of Valois, and is also divided into three parts: into the True, the Lower and the Upper. The True begins at Crèvecœur, containing the Vidamé of Amiens, Corbie, Picquigny, the Counties of Vermandois and of Rethelois, and the Duchy of Thiérache, of which Guise is the principal town. Lower Picardy begins at the country of Santerre, following True France and the Duchy of Valois, comprising the Counties of Ponthieu, of Montreuil, of Guînes, of the Boulonnais, and the country of Oye. Upper Picardy takes its beginning beyond the river Somme, and contains the Cambrésis, the Tournaisis, the Counties of Hainaut and of Namur, the country of Trier, the duchies of Luxembourg and of Brabant; indeed the County of Flanders was anciently of Upper Picardy. But whence Picardy may have drawn its name is still uncertain, unless we choose to follow the conjecture of some who — just as the Lombards are so called from their having been wont to wear long beards, or else because from them first came the use of the long javelins called *bardes* — so think the Picards to have been so named because from this people came the beginning of the use of pikes.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOTAT. CII. on p144; the transcription numbers it 101. It expounds the word *Picardie* of the confession (texte-99): a geographical excursus in the humanist manner, with no marginal citations. One page break, at *La Tranſalpine | eſtoit nommée* (p144/p145), kept at "The Transalpine ⟦p145⟧was the name". Five `⟨alt⟩` markers, listed in the report; none changes the English (each is a stray accent in one reading). Reflow gap read through: *Ra uenne* (*Rauenne*). *Suquoy* is *Surquoy* ("Whereupon"). Place-names are given in their modern forms, with Coras's in the report where they differ: *Riminy* Rimini, *Comenge* Comminges, *Garomne* Garonne, *Rhein* Rhine, *Creuecœur* Crèvecœur, *Piquigny* Picquigny, *Retelois* Rethelois, *Tierache* Thiérache, *Guyſe* Guise, *Montreul* Montreuil, *Guynes* Guînes, *Boulenois* Boulonnais, *Torneſis* Tournaisis, *Treues* Trier, *Luxembour* Luxembourg. *Marneuf* is kept as Coras has it (the Marne rises at Balesmes-sur-Marne, near Langres; the village name is Coras's). *Germains, que nous appellons Allemans* → "the Germans, whom we call *Allemans*" — the French word kept, since the sentence is about the name. *Vidamie* → "Vidamé" (the jurisdiction of the *vidame*, the bishop's lay deputy); *la vraye France* → "True France" (the Île-de-France, *France* in the narrow sense, as *la vraye Picardie* is Picardy proper); *le pays d'Oye* → "the country of Oye" (the Pays d'Oye between Calais and Gravelines). *Longues Iauelines de Barde* → "the long javelins called *bardes*" — the etymology of *Lombard* from *barde*, a weapon, as against *longue barbe*; the word is kept because the argument is about the name. Coras's "True, Lower and Upper Picardy", with Upper Picardy reaching to Hainaut, Namur, Trier, Luxembourg and Brabant, is the geographers' Picardy of his day, not a modern province; it is translated as he gives it, without correction.

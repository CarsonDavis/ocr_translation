⟦p133⟧Above, we have sufficiently, as it seems to me, proved {a} that this daughter (now the sole survivor of the two children procreated by the doings of the said du Tilh and de Rols) was legitimate, by reason of the error and good faith of the said de Rols {b}; as also, in like manner, we say of the children who should be born of a priest whom the woman had married, thinking that he was a lay person and fit to marry, that for the ignorance and good faith of the woman they are legitimate {c}. Wherefore it must not be doubted that such children succeed to father, mothers and their other kinsfolk {d}, and not only in rural and patrimonial goods, but also in fiefs and other noble goods {e}. And, to be brief, such children must in all and every respect be deemed no less legitimate than if they had been procreated, conceived and begotten of a just and legitimate ⟦p134⟧marriage {f}; and so that they succeed indifferently to all: that is to say, as much to the one who had contracted the marriage unwittingly and in good faith as to the one also who had used fraud and known of the impediment, and to the common kinsfolk also, respectively {g}. And although some have rather lightly thought that such children are legitimate only with regard to the one alone who contracted through error and in good faith {h}, nevertheless this opinion has long since, and with great reason, been rejected: for it would be a thing far too ridiculous that a child should be deemed in part legitimate, and in part bastard and illegitimate {i}. And as to the argument that between the said du Tilh and de Rols there was no true marriage, without which children cannot be called legitimate {k}: the answer is easy, that the colour and belief of marriage, as to making the children legitimate, has the same force and the same effects as a just, true and perfect marriage {l}; as also we say that a true possession of a piece of land, or of other things, is well and justly acquired, although the delivery of that thing, and the means of the acquisition, be not real {m}, but feigned and imaginary. And if someone says here that, although this daughter is legitimate, one ought not for all that to have adjudged to her the goods of her father condemned to death, inasmuch as such a condemnation draws to itself the confiscation of goods {n}, especially in France, where he who confiscates the body confiscates the goods {o}: I answer that although the Judge who has the power to confiscate the body, that is to say to condemn it to death, may also confiscate the goods, nevertheless one must not therefore think that he is perpetually bound to do so {p}. For such a circumstance may arise from the facts, and present itself to the judge, that he may justly not confiscate the goods, in whole or in part, and especially when there are children {q}. True it is that in France the judge must expressly declare it: for otherwise the mere favour of the children would not prevent the condemnation to death from necessarily carrying with it also the confiscation of the goods {r}.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOTAT. XCV. on p133; it expounds *adiuge les biens dudit du Tilh, à la fille…* (texte-93) and runs to p134. The body carries seventeen markers, {a}–{r} (no *j*), each served by a keyed note. The `⟨alt⟩` choices in this section, all in the margin, are recorded in the report: *agna.⟨alt:agn.⟩*, *ri.⟨alt:rit.⟩*, *ſol.⟨alt:ſol⟩* and *q⟨alt:ꝗ⟩* are variant abbreviations of the same words (*agnatos*, *ritu*, *soluto*, *qui*); *uĩcia⟨alt:uicia⟩* is taken with the tilde (*prouincia*); *ij.⟨alt:iij.⟩* in note *h* as *ij.*, the number annot-011 gives for the same law; *Accurſe⟨alt:Accurſè⟩* as *Accurſe*; *bona.⟨alt:bova.⟩* as *bona* (the Authentica *Bona damnatorum*).

⟦p051⟧Although sometimes men shed tears from a great and too excessive joy which suddenly presents itself, as did Ptolemy Philadelphus when there were offered to him the laws of the Jews written in letters of gold {a2}; nevertheless it most often happens that man weeps only from melancholy, vexation and sadness, and then there is great reason for it: for the spirit, which is extremely afflicted with grief and anguish, is carried up as far as the membrane of the brain, where it presses the humour that it finds there, so that it constrains it to go out; and that is why the Latins call the humour that flows down from the eyes LACRIMA, that is to say tear: a word drawn from *laceration*, which is as much as to say to break, to rend and to consume; for from the breaking and sadness of the spirit the tears are moved and provoked. Alexander of Aphrodisias ⟦p052⟧for that reason assigns the reason in both, that is to say in those who grieve and in those who rejoice, and also why both the one and the other shed tears and weep: for in those who grieve, this proceeds, he says, from the thickness of the little conduits of the sight, which comes to press the humour of the eyes; and those who make merry, for the rarity [openness] of those passages and conduits, pour out such moisture {b}

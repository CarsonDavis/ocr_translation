⟦p060⟧The testimony of kinsfolk or relatives by marriage, and especially of fathers, mothers, children, brothers and sisters, sons-in-law and brothers-in-law, for the great affection they naturally bear to their own and so near blood, is not, nor by reason ought to be, received {a}, unless in certain cases discussed elsewhere by our interpreters, of which two or three only serve us. The first, when it would be a question of proving a thing in which the faith of kinsfolk would be more required than that of all others {b}: as to prove age, or to recognise (which is our case) whether someone is of their kinship or affinity {c}. The second, when the kinsman would not be produced by the party who is his kin, but would have been taken (as in this present case) by the judge on his own motion {d}. The third, when his deposition would profit the kinsfolk and harm no one: as when the witness would depose (as in this case the said witnesses do) for the innocence of a kinsman of his, even were he his own brother, nay, and one accused of heresy {e}. Whence several have thought and taught that one accused of homi⟦p061⟧cide may produce his own brother against the fisc [the royal treasury], or the King, who alone is the party against him, to show that he committed the killing in defending himself {f}: for although the King pursues the confiscation of the accused's goods, nevertheless such a penal and odious gain, which cannot come about except with the detriment and loss both of the honour and of the life of the accused, does not come into consideration. {g}

The marker alphabet restarts at *a* and runs a–g across p060–p061; every note is keyed and every marker served; the print sets {g} after the final full stop and it is kept there.

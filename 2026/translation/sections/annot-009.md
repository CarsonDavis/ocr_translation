⟦p023⟧From this fact a question may arise in the future: whether the true husband, coming upon the scene, may retract the alienations of his property, and not only the parcels sold, but may also recover the fruits gathered and received by the purchasers since the time of the contracts. For the decision of which doubt, it must be presupposed that anyone may freely sell not only his own property, of which he is master and owner, but also the property of another; and the sale is good and valid {d} — in so far, however, as concerns the prejudice of the seller, who by this contract is bound to deliver the thing sold, or to pay damages, and further, if the parcel is evicted by a third party, to warrant and indemnify the purchaser {e}. But to the detri⟦p024⟧ment of the true owner and master of the parcel sold, the contract has no force; for what is ours cannot be taken from us without our will {g}. And certainly it would be a thing far too iniquitous and unreasonable that another should retain and enjoy my property against my will {h}. Added to which, neither by the delivery of a thing nor by any agreement can more right be transferred than he who delivered it had in it {i}, whether by testamentary dispositions, {k} gifts, sales or other contracts {l}. Therefore, to our purpose, it must undoubtedly be believed that the contracts made by this cheat du Tilh can in no way prejudice Martin Guerre, who without difficulty will recover from the purchasers the lands themselves. But as to the fruits, they will remain with the said purchasers, provided however that they bought, and have always possessed, in good faith, that is to say, thinking that the said du Tilh, the seller, was Martin Guerre. And to understand it clearly, it is to be considered that the good faith of him who buys, or otherwise contracts, with one whom everyone thought to be the true owner and master of the thing, produces two singular and notable effects. The first: that he who possesses the parcel by title of purchase, or the like, and in good faith — that is to say, thinking that he from whom he had the parcel, by purchase or otherwise, was its true master — will gain the fruits of the parcel so long as he holds it with this good faith; for as to the fruits, he stands in the place of the owner and represents him {m}. The second effect: that he might possess it so long with this good faith that in time he would prescribe it — that is to say, would acquire it in ownership, and irrevocably, by long usage {n}: unless the contract of sale should by chance have been made of the property of a ward {o}, or against the prohibition of the law or of the testator {p}, in which case the purchaser (though he had more good faith and probity than a Scipio Nasica) could not acquire the parcel by prescription, or by possession however long it might be {q}. Now there is no doubt that he is called in our law a possessor in good faith who has title either from the man he believes to be the owner of the parcel, or from his attorney, guardian or curator {r}.

⟦p130⟧Rigour would certainly have commanded that he be burnt alive {a}; but to obviate some despair which might have overcome the temerity of this impostor, frantic and full of rage, the Court ordered that he should first be strangled. Wherein all judges shall be admonished not to exercise, without some great and notable cause, those cruel and brutal ferocities of burning or dismembering malefactors while fully alive. In which carnage, nevertheless, some who are bloodthirsty, inhumanly unnatural, and born, as it seems, to all rigour and ferocity, take such pleasure that one would never see them at ease or content except when they have thus fought against nature, and cruelly shed, by new and unheard-of punishments, the blood of their neighbour: and sometimes so furiously that the most barbarous and cruel tyrants would have horror of performing like acts. Which proceeds most often from a brutal nature, but sometimes from ignorance or from wickedness: for (besides that

Than an unskilled man, nothing is ever more unjust (*Homine imperito nunquam quicquam iniustius*) {b}),

just as the unskilled, under this pretext, think to cover their imperfection and ignorance, so the wicked also believe ⟦p131⟧no less that, under this mantle of rigour and severity of penalty, they wholly efface, or at least in part purge, and clean a little, the filth of their evil life, their corruptions and detestable vices: without thinking that we are all Christians, and children of one heavenly Father, regenerated by one Baptism, and by it incorporated into the Church of God, redeemed by the one blood of Jesus Christ, our head, of whom we are all members, and under whose banner we battle against Satan, the common enemy of nature, who surrounds us day and night to devour us; wherefore, if he catches one of us, all ought to try to bear his burden and his load, by pity, gentleness, compassion and mercy {c}. And we ought moreover to consider that with judgments it is as with victories, of which that which is won without the shedding of human blood is always the noblest and the most acceptable before God. Not that I would thereby take away the sword, which I well know to be given to magistrates for the vengeance of malefactors, and to do justice in wrath (says the Apostle) upon him that does evil {d}. For I am not ignorant that, as Publilius the Mime-writer has left written: Whosoever spares the wicked, harms the good.

*Bonis nocet, quisquis pepercerit malis* [He harms the good who spares the wicked].

And that the sharpness and severity of penalties is sometimes the teaching and discipline of living well. {e} But I mean that this must be done with great circumspection and prudence, and that judges must not readily make themselves prodigal of the blood of their Christian brother, but rather exercise themselves more in humanity and clemency than in rigour and cruelty — and the sovereign judges especially: both because there is nothing so ugly, so base, nor so deformed as to add to a sovereign power a sharpness and acerbity of nature {f}, and because also they ought to serve as examples to their inferiors and, like lamps, give light to all the others. Wherefore, when it shall be fitting to put the malefactor to death for his demerits, let it be without the horror and confusion of these cruel and barbarous spectacles, unless it be for some great, horrible and enor⟦p132⟧mous deed, as against Sodomites, Atheists and brigands, who, being so frantic and enraged as to study nothing but inhuman and unnatural things, well deserve also to be restrained by inhumanity and ferocity of penalty {g}. But let us recover our breath a little, and the track of our path. Someone perhaps will say here that the Court was necessarily bound to condemn the said du Tilh to be burnt alive, and not after his death {h}; inasmuch as, where the law commands that some malefactor be burnt, he must be burnt fully alive {i}. Added to which, after the death of the accused all prosecution of crimes ends and is extinguished {k}, so that several have written that judges who have the dead bodies and carcasses of executed men fastened or hung upon the gibbet-forks do things unworthy of them, and err greatly {l}. To resolve which difficulty, I shall confess in the first place that to vex, torment and punish the body of a dead person, whom God has called to himself and to the judgment of his great tribunal, is a very strange thing, and one smacking of I know not what of the barbarity and inhumanity {m} with which that great name of Achilles remains still stained, when, to avenge the death of Patroclus his great friend (whom Hector son of Priam had killed), not content with having slain Hector, he had his body tied to a chariot and dragged three times around Troy and the tomb of Patroclus {n}.

Three times around the walls of Ilium he had dragged Hector (*Ter circum Iliacos raptaverat Hectora muros*) {o}.

The horror and enormity of the crime may nevertheless be so great that, in detestation of it, it belongs to the example and to the greatness of a well-ordered Commonwealth to punish, roast and dismember the bodies, the carcasses, of the dead: so that the memory of persons so wretched and abominable may be wholly annihilated and lost, and that by the spectacle of such a penalty these prodigiously wicked men may be frightened and turned away from like evil deeds {q}. Thus our Accursius admonishes us to do to brigands and highway robbers, after they are hanged and strangled: namely, to expose them to famished beasts, to be torn to pieces and devoured by them {r}. And inasmuch as it would not be easy to enumerate ⟦p133⟧or set out the crimes which, for their enormity, deserve the prodigy of such a penalty, this is committed and left to the discretion of the judge {ſ}: who, if he sees it expedient to exercise the sword of justice upon the carcass of a man executed to death, may justly and indubitably do so {t}; singularly in France, where the judges take cognizance of crimes only extraordinarily {u}. Wherefore it ought not to be found so novel or strange that, in horror and execration of a crime, the body of a dead person should be punished {x}. As by the same reason also the law sometimes commands, in detestation of a horrible crime of brutish sodomy, that the brute beasts be punished and cruelly put to death {y}. And on this subject our canons (much milder and more gracious in penalties than the civil laws) have nevertheless willed that if a religious who has made a vow of poverty is discovered after his death to have possessed any property as his own, his body must be dug out of the sepulchre and thrown on a dunghill, or else into a privy {z}.

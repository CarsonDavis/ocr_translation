⟦p005⟧This long duration and lengthy absence of the husband made him, at first sight, greatly culpable, and almost beyond all excuse, as having given the occasion for the misfortune and disaster which has since followed from it: witness Saint Augustine, when he says, IF YOU abstain for long without the will of your wife, you give her licence to fornicate: and if she goes astray, her sin will be imputed to your abstinence {a}. Whence some have even dared to say that a Judge might justly absolve a wife accused by her husband of adultery, if she is not convicted of having squandered her honour and abandoned herself except after having been long forsaken by her husband {b}; inasmuch as the husband, who has furnished the occasions, and has thereby made himself guilty of the misdeed {c}, cannot upbraid her with it, nor object it against his wife {d}. As in a like case also, the husband, who by the old laws could repudiate his consort if without his leave she had slept outside the house {e}, nevertheless, if he himself had driven her out, and on that occasion she remained all night outside, cannot repudiate her, because (says Justinian) he himself is the author of this fault {f}. But if this opinion were true, women, licentious enough of themselves, might seize upon a great pretext to prostitute themselves with impunity, covering themselves with the cloak of the husband's absence: to which, so far from our being bound to lend a hand, on the contrary everyone ought to reject this opinion as impious, unworthy of a Christian, and so barbarous that even the Gentiles and Pagans did not think it good that a wife (whose husband had remained long absent) should remarry, until after having heard ⟦p006⟧certain news of the husband's death {g}. Of which the chaste Penelope once engraved a holy and eternally memorable example upon the tablets of posterity. For during the absence of Ulysses her spouse (which was, however, of twenty years), she could never be overcome by the continual entreaties of her kinsfolk, nor persuaded, by the urgent solicitations of countless young men (who sought to have her to wife), to remarry.

Penelope was able to live untouched through twice ten years, a woman worthy of so many suitors (*Penelope poterat, bis denos salva per annos, / vivere, tam multis foemina digna procis*). {h}

And because, besides the excellent and native beauty with which nature had happily enriched her, she was further commended by I know not what ray of virtue, gentleness and simplicity, which made her lovable and admirable in the eyes of all, she was violently pressed, and solicited almost by force, by her suitors: to repulse whom graciously, she obtained from them a delay until she should have finished weaving the little piece of cloth that she had in hand (which they granted her), hoping to see the cloth soon woven: but she, having her heart always with her Ulysses, to cheat these gentle lovers of their foolish hope, unravelled by night what she had woven by day: and thus kept them waiting and deceived them until the return of her consort {i}. Wherefore one must not be so impious as to think that, if the wife had prostituted herself during the husband's absence (and he for that reason has driven her out), and she asked to be reinstated in her marriage, the adultery would not justly be objected against her {k}: unless the husband had lent a hand to his wife's misconduct, and to that end had deliberately absented himself {l}. For although that does not excuse the wife, who for anything in the world ought not to be so liberal with her honour, which she ought to hold a thousand times dearer than life {m}: nevertheless it charges the husband, and makes him so far culpable that he would without difficulty be punished as his wife's pander; so far is it from his being able to object or reproach such a fault against her in judgment {n}. And if anyone here asks how long a wife is obliged to wait for her ab⟦p007⟧sent husband (of whom it is uncertain whether he is alive or dead) before she may think of a second marriage: I shall answer that if all women had as good a will as Penelope (whose history we have related), or as Porcia, daughter of Cato, who said that the woman who remarries is neither chaste nor modest, and had the intention of saying with Dido {o}:

He who first joined me to himself took my loves away with him; let him keep them with him, and guard them in the tomb (*Ille meos primus, qui me sibi iunxit, amores / abstulit: ille habeat secum, servetque sepulchro*).

we should have no great trouble in settling this question. But since not all have such a gift of continence, nor the power to tame and vanquish with such great strength the passion of the flesh, and since after the husband's death God by His holy law permits every woman to remarry whom she will {p}: I say, to the point of our question, that although the civil law in one place was content to make the wife wait five years {q}, nevertheless the Emperor Justinian and the Pontiffs of Rome have willed that, even though thirty years had passed, if there is no certain news of the husband, it is not lawful for the wife to remarry {r}. True it is that the same authors well understand that the news shall be found certain enough (the husband having died in the war) if the captain under whom the husband followed arms sent a certificate of his death, or himself in person attested it {ſ}. Or else, if the husband was drowned, by proving that the ship in which he was has foundered. Indeed it would suffice to show by witnesses, or otherwise, that the rumour of his death is spread abroad everywhere {u}. For although proof by rumour and renown, according to the ordinary and common rules, is not conclusive, nor received {u}, nevertheless, where one cannot easily obtain true and certain proof, as when the husband had remained in a distant country, long absent, such a manner of proof, by rumour and common repute, would suffice {y}.

⟦p027⟧Because names are given or imposed only to distinguish and recognise persons, {a} it is lawful for anyone, of his own free will, to take the name that seems good to him, and having taken it, to change it freely afterwards, provided that it is without fraud, and that the change is not to the detriment or damage of another {b}. For where the intention was bad, to defraud one's neighbour or to harm him in some way, it would then be a crime of forgery {c}, and thus punishable by death, civil death at the least {d}. As for the substitution of persons, one cannot well give certain rules for it: for the ancients sometimes punished it, sometimes not; and when they punished it, sometimes sharply and with natural death, sometimes lightly, as we shall discourse below, God helping, in a more convenient place. {e} Meanwhile, however, it will not be out of place to understand that in our law another kind of substitution is spoken of, namely when a wife substitutes in her husband's house a child as her own, which is nevertheless another's, to make it heir to the goods of her husband {f} — a crime certainly grave, and surely repressed by the law, which was pleased to punish it not only in the person of the wife who had contrived and plotted the substitution, but also in all those who had given her counsel, favour and aid {g}; and although in other crimes some manner of impunity is gained by the passing of years {h}, nevertheless in this crime, he who is guilty does not escape the penalty by any lapse or interval of time whatsoever {i}. True also that the pen⟦p028⟧alty for this crime, although the Emperor makes it capital {k}, is nevertheless not among the most certain, inasmuch as a capital penalty may refer both to civil death and to natural death {l}. Wherein I have always been of opinion to leave and commit to the discretion of the Judge the kind of death, who, having weighed the qualities of the persons and balanced all the circumstances, may lighten or aggravate the penalty {m}: singularly in our France, where cognizance is taken of crimes only extraordinarily; in which case the gravity and lightness of penalties seem to depend entirely upon the Judge {n}.

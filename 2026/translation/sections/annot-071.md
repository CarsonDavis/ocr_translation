⟦p090⟧There was certainly great reason to think that this accused had some familiar spirit, seeing that he knew so well and truly how to answer of all things, even the most secret and private, without ever failing by a nail's breadth. And (what is far more to be wondered at) he knew all those who presented themselves to him, at the beginning and afterwards, without ever having seen them. Which could not fall within instructions or memoranda that had been given him by another; and particularly since he had never (that anyone knew) been at the place of Artigat, nor conversed with the inhabitants of that place. And it is not to be doubted that, among the prodigious and abominable tyrannies which Satan, since the creation of the world, has cruelly exercised against men, to ensnare them and draw them into his kingdom, he has kept a great storehouse of magic, opened the shop of such merchandise, and dealt it out to countless men so liberally that he has ⟦p091⟧made himself revered by many with great wonder; persuading them, moreover, that all things were feasible by means of magical vanity {a}. And yet neither must one think that magic is altogether fabulous, seeing that the laws, both divine and human, have so often spoken of it, and commanded that it be exterminated from the earth as a thing abominable and full of impiety {b}. These were certainly among the first ruses and principal wiles that this trickster and father of lies, Satan, set up to ruin and seduce men: even to having Simon the Samaritan adored as God, and a statue erected to him with this inscription, SIMONI DEO SANCTO, which is as much as to say, To Simon the holy God {c}. Now this diabolical art (the invention of which is attributed to Zoroaster, King of the Bactrians, who wrote a hundred thousand verses {d}) is called Magic; although Magic of itself signifies nothing evil, but rather all sapience, wisdom and knowledge of things universally, both human and divine. But two kinds have been made of it: the one natural and permitted, the other ceremonial and reprobate. Natural Magic is an excellent learning and perfect knowledge of the secret virtues of nature, whether in celestial influences, stones or herbs; to learn which Pythagoras, Empedocles and Democritus voluntarily banished themselves from their native land, wandered as vagabonds through divers provinces, and travelled into far-off and foreign countries; and such Magic was of old in those noble and renowned wise men who came from the East to adore the little child Jesus Christ {e}; in Archytas of Tarentum also, who had made a dove of wood, balanced with counterweights, of such a structure and so ingenious a contrivance that it flew in the air of itself; moreover in Boethius, who made birds sing, and cows and bulls, made of metal, bellow {f}; and in Albert the Great, who made a head of brass speak; and other such. Ceremonial Magic is so called because it consists wholly in superstitions and ceremonies of words, names, images, characters, consecrations, sacrifices and other like vanities {g}, by which the ⟦p092⟧professors of such ravings boast that they can procure spirits, and by their juggleries and illusions do all things, even to drawing down the stars from heaven; witness Virgil, when he says {h}:

Charms can even draw down the Moon from heaven (*Carmina vel coelo possunt deducere Lunam*).

Of this magic some make two kinds, Goety and Theurgy; others (as Marcus Varro) four: Necromancy, Pyromancy, Aeromancy and Hydromancy {i}. The more recent add to these four Geomancy and Chiromancy {k}. Necromancy and Goety are all one, signifying the art of divining by the invocation of the spirits of the dead: called Necromancy from νεκρός, which means dead, and μαντεία, divination {l}; and Goety from *planctus* (wailing), for the mourning that must be made about the sepulchre from which one wishes to draw and invoke the shade {m}. Or else, and most often, this pernicious art is practised by the invocation of unclean and evil spirits: as when Pharaoh's enchanters turned rods into serpents and the waters of the rivers into blood {n}, and the Pythoness of Endor, at the request of King Saul, made the soul of Samuel come by her familiar spirit {o} — for, as many interpret, it was not the soul of the Prophet, but of some evil spirit which had taken his form {p}, or else a spectre or phantom. From Goety Theurgy differs in this, that Theurgy is practised by invocation of God and of the good spirits, such as the Angels; by which, by means of the abstinences and other ceremonies required, many have thought they could draw to themselves the celestial powers and divine virtues: which is attributed to Apollonius of Tyana, of whom marvellous things are related — namely, that he understood the jargon of birds; could tell all that was being done in the strangest and most far-removed countries of the world; spoke all tongues without ever having been taught; declared the thoughts of men; foretold things to come; and (what is above all astonishing, and what a Christian ought not easily to believe) raised the dead — which he boasted he did by the counsel of God and the ⟦p093⟧guidance of His Spirit {q}. Thus Socrates procured his daemon; and Iamblichus has left particular writings and treatises on it, to teach the means of having such spirits {r}. Great ravings and dangerous illusions, which St Augustine has learnedly and amply recounted {ſ}: for they are nothing but the juggleries, enchantments and trickeries of that great father of lies, Satan, who very often transfigures himself into an Angel of light to deceive men {t}. Pyromancy is divination by fire, as when one observes what the flames of the fire, the lightnings, thunders or thunderbolts signify {u}; which Virgil touches on when he says,

I remember that oaks struck from heaven foretold it (*De coelo tactas memini praedicere quercus*).

The example of it is also at hand in Tanaquil, wife of Tarquinius Priscus, fifth King of the Romans, who, seeing a flame of fire surround the head of a poor child called Servius Tullius, foretold thereby that he would be King of Rome {x}. Cicero in a certain place shows that, by the discipline of the Etruscans, if from a fire there came a double flame, or the flame at its point divided in two, that presaged quarrels and dissensions {y}. Under Pyromancy may justly be comprised Capnomancy, which is a kind of divination made by the smoke of the fire: for if it turns in a circle it signifies one thing; if it goes crosswise, or bends, or else stretches straight upward, it presages another {z}. Aeromancy is divination by the air, as by the flight and song of birds at liberty, by rains, tempests and unaccustomed storms. Thus when it rained stones in the March of Ancona (which the Latins always called Picenum), there was signified the defeat and carnage that Hannibal made of the Romans in Italy {a}. Hydromancy is made by inspection, and the invocation of spirits, in water {b}: as when a young child (of whom Marcus Varro speaks) saw in the water Mercury, who recited in a hundred and fifty verses all that came to pass in the war of Mithridates {c}. From Hydromancy Lecanomancy is not very different, which is a kind of divination made in ⟦p094⟧a basin full of water, where with certain charms one makes a spirit come, which at first quivers and leaps about in the water, and afterwards, hissing, utters a little voice, by which it answers what is asked of it {d}. Geomancy is a divination which was practised of old, before the use of paper and ink had been found, by points cast on the earth; whence it took, and still keeps, its name; but now it is no longer practised on the earth, but on white paper or some other thing apt to receive the points and lines, from which the judgment is afterwards made. Chiromancy is divination made by inspection of the lines of the hand. Besides the aforesaid kinds of Magic, some set down another, which is called Pharmacy {e} — wrongly, however, in my opinion, seeing that it does not consist solely in drugs, potions and poisonings, to make one die, or love, or else hate: as when, in the case of Faustina, daughter of Antoninus and wife of Marcus Antoninus the Philosopher and Emperor, to make her lose the measureless love she bore to I know not what gladiator, for love of whom she was dying, [unclear: the print wants its main verb here — Capitolinus has the gladiator killed and Faustina bathed in his blood] and immediately after to lie with her husband; which she did, and so lost her love for that fine swordsman — true it is that she was that very hour got with child of Antoninus Commodus, a prince who was afterwards so cruel and bloodthirsty that he better deserved the name and title of gladiator than of Emperor or prince {f}. And although such potions are given for love, yet they are very dangerous, and from them there often follows either death or an extreme frenzy {g}: as in Lucretius (that great and excellent Poet), who, after eating what Lucilia his too-jealous wife had prepared for him, to draw him to her love, became so frenzied that he killed himself {h}. That is why Ovid strongly dissuades from the use of such love-charms, saying {i}:

Nor will pale philtres given to girls avail; philtres harm the mind, and carry the force of madness (*Nec data profuerint pallentia philtra puellis; philtra nocent animis, vimque furoris habent*).

I do not however wish to deny that, when from morsels or potions some prodigious effect should follow, Pharmacy might be placed among the ⟦p095⟧kinds of Magic: as when Demaenetus of Parrhasia (or, if you will believe Pausanias {k}, Demarchus), having tasted of the sacrifice of a young child which the Arcadians made to Lycaeus their God, was turned into a wolf, and at the end of ten years took again the form of a man {l}. Whereof St Augustine learnedly disputes {m}, and shows these things to be no less fabulous than what is written of the companions of Diomedes, King of Aetolia, who after the destruction of Troy were transmuted into birds; and of Circe, who is feigned to have transmuted Scylla (of whom she was jealous) into a sea-monster, and the companions of Ulysses into swine.

With her charms Circe transformed the companions of Ulysses (*Carminibus Circe socios mutavit Ulixis*) {n}.

Among such prodigies we approve only the history of Nebuchadnezzar, King of Babylon, who by the will of God was transmuted into an ox, and remained so seven years among the other beasts, eating grass; and afterwards, by the mercy of God, the figure of a man was restored to him {o}. Not that I would altogether deny the conversion among human beings from one sex to the other: for besides the examples recounted by Pliny and Gellius {p}, there is reason plain enough that this can be done without Magic or any artifice; for man and woman have the instruments for begetting altogether alike, save that the man's extends outward and the woman's inward, and that the testicles (or, if you prefer, the genitories) do not hang in women. Nothing is needed, then, to bring about the said conversion, but that by some accident of sickness, or otherwise, the man's member withdraw within the body, and he will become a woman; or that the woman's advance outward, and behold a man {q}. True it is that I do not remember ever to have read an example of a man being transmuted into a woman, but only of women, that they are sometimes transformed into men: wherein nature shows her clemency and benignity, in not willing to change things for the worse, but always for the better. There are several other kinds of Magic, which there is no need to write of here, especial⟦p096⟧ly as all are vain and ridiculous, proceeding from the wiles of Satan, and rejected not only by the express commandment of God, repeated in a hundred places, under threat of death {r}, but also by the human laws (of which, nevertheless, the greater part of the authors were heathens), under the like penalty {ſ}. This most great, most good and most mighty God has given us the word of His Gospel, to which in our adversities we may withdraw, take counsel and be comforted; and not inquire into things that do not belong to us — as Abraham answered, holding Lazarus in his bosom, to the wicked Rich Man who was in the torments of hell and begged him to send Lazarus to his brothers: They have Moses (said Abraham) and the Prophets; if your brothers will not believe them, neither will they believe though one of the dead should rise again {t}. Let us not permit, then, that Satan — who raises his horns day and night, and spreads these dangerous fowling-nets to entangle us by this means in his meshes, from which Jesus Christ, Son of the living God, has so dearly redeemed us by the incomprehensible pains and torments of His passion — should impose upon us in this matter and seduce us; but in such or like temptation let us always withdraw to our Redeemer, and most humbly beseech Him that He will set our hearts aright and lead us in His ways, so that by the light of His word we may drive from us all illusions, juggleries and impostures (with which the devil, who seeks always to catch us, incessantly lays new ambushes against the children of God and His Church).

⟦p074⟧Frequent experience will keep the reader from being astonished here: for daily we see many men and women slender, thin and delicate in their youth, who nevertheless, by the passage of time and the advancement of age, become stout, fat and cumbersome. And because the examples of this time might be odious to some, I shall have recourse to the most memorable of antiquity. Leo of Byzantium, the sophist, very lean in his first years, became so fat about his middle age that, being ambassador at Athens and mounted upon a high chair to appease some sedition, all the people, seeing him so excessively big and pot-bellied, began to laugh; whereupon Leo began to speak thus: WHAT IS IT that you laugh at, O Athenians, to see me so fat? Know that my wife is fatter still. And yet, when we are agreed, we two can well [lie] in a little bed; but on the contrary, when we are angry, the whole house is not enough for us. Which speech had so much grace and force with this ⟦p075⟧people that it was forthwith appeased, and the sedition ceased. Dionysius of Heraclea, the tyrant, having been slender in his young years, became little by little so monstrously fat, paunchy and pot-bellied that he was constrained at night to apply a great quantity of leeches to his body, to suck out of him the excessive superfluity of the humour which made him so fat and corpulent. I leave aside Louis the Fat, thirty-ninth King of France, who flourished from the year 1110 to the year 1137, who became so immoderately big and fat that he carried away from it the surname of the Fat.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOTAT. LIIII. on p074; it carries no marginal citations. The page break falls between *ce* and *peuple* and is kept at the corresponding words ("this ⟦p075⟧people"). Wrong sorts and slips translated by sense: *biens* for *bien* ("nous pouuons biens tous deux"); *recouuray* for *recourray* (*recourir à*, "to have recourse to"), unless Coras means *recouvrer*, "to fetch back"; the sense is the same. Reflow gaps read through: *ieu neſſe*, *auance mẽt*, *ceſ ſa*, *ſuc cer*. *Greſles, linges, & dolietz* → "slender, thin and delicate" (*linge*, the old adjective "thin, slight"; *doliet* = *douillet*, "soft, delicate"); *gros, gras, & importuns* → "stout, fat and cumbersome" (*importun* in its physical sense, burdensome in bulk); *ſur ſon mediocre aage* → "about his middle age"; *monté ſur vne haute chaire* → "mounted upon a high chair" (the speaker's platform); *gros & ventru* → "big and pot-bellied"; *ſe print à rire* → "began to laugh"; the verb of "nous pouuons biens tous deux dans vn petit lict" is elided in the French and supplied in brackets ("[lie]"); *courroucez* → "angry"; *incontinent* → "forthwith"; *panſard & ventru* → "paunchy and pot-bellied"; *ſangſues* → "leeches"; *l'exceſſiue ſuperfluité de l'humeur* → "the excessive superfluity of the humour" (the medical sense); *il en rapporta le ſurnom du Gros* → "he carried away from it the surname of the Fat". The capitals of QV'EST-CE are the print's and are kept. The anecdotes are uncited in the margin: Leo of Byzantium's retort to the laughing Athenians is in Plutarch, *Precepts of Statecraft* (*Moralia* 804a), and Athenaeus, *Deipnosophists* 12.550f (where the sedition is at Byzantium and the embassy is to Athens, as Coras has it); the fatness of Dionysius, tyrant of Heraclea Pontica, is in Athenaeus 12.549a–b and Aelian, *Varia historia* 9.13, where the physicians use long needles rather than leeches; Louis VI the Fat reigned 1108–1137, and Coras's "thirty-ninth King of France" follows the traditional count of the old chronicles from Pharamond. All three are left as Coras gives them.

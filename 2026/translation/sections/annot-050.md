⟦p071⟧As for the marriage, it is in the first place certain that, in doubtful things, the law wills and commands that judgment be given [for it] {a}; so that our interpreters say that this presumption, which one ought to take in favour of marriage, conquers and overcomes almost all the others {b}. In the second place, the favour of the children who are issued of this cohabitation is of great weight for rendering them entirely legitimate: for although, for the ignorance and good faith of the said de Rols, by a most equitable interpretation of our canons such children may be said and deemed legitimate {c}, nevertheless, if we go by the truth of the matter [unclear: the print's *ſi nous ignorons*, perhaps for *iugeons*], they are not so, being undoubtedly born of lechery and begotten of adultery {d}. Thirdly, the cause of the accused has at all times been so favoured that the authors of our laws often admonish us to answer for them in controverted facts, and such as admit of some doubt, and to be more inclined to deliver and absolve the accused than to condemn them {e}; nay, even and singularly in public and capital crimes {f}, in which honour and life are at stake, and even though the proofs of the plaintiff and of the accuser should somewhat surpass the proofs of the defendant {g}. Of which one may, with Aristotle, render two or three reasons {h}. The first, that it is not in the power of the defendant to plead the cause, or to come into judgment, when he sees fit, as it is in the power of the plaintiff or of the accuser to sue or to accuse at his will {i}; whence it may be that he, being pressed to answer, has forgotten the principal point of his defence, and of the justice of his cause. The second, because just as a pilgrim, in travelling, ought always to choose and take the surer road or path {k}, so in doubtful and perplexed affairs the judge ought always to embrace and follow the opinion which has less danger and peril {l}. And no one doubts that it is incomparably surer to leave the guilty unpunished than to condemn the inno⟦p072⟧cent {m}; and so to embrace the gentler, more humane opinion, and that which tends to the deliverance of the accused {n}.

⟦p055⟧Although the language of the Basques is very obscure and ⟦p056⟧so difficult that several have thought it could not be expressed by any characters of letters: nevertheless it is not likely that a native Basque should not know how to speak his language: for to be ignorant of, or to have forgotten, one's native tongue [*ramage*] cannot proceed but either from foolishness and silliness, or from an accident of illness, or from old age. From stolidity and foolishness: as with one Amphistides, so dull and idiotic that he could not say whether he had been born of a father or of a mother; and Atticus, son of Herodes the Sophist, who was so silly and dull-witted that he could never learn, nor retain, the names of the letters. By accident of some great illness: as one Messala Corvinus, an excellent orator, whom the remains of a strong and vehement illness made forget his own name; and certain others, for having fallen from the top of a house, or received some blows of stones, [it made] forget their letters, and fail to recognise their nearest kinsfolk and friends {a}. By extreme old age and decrepitude: as with Francesco Barbaro, who in his decaying years forgot the Greek letters, in which before he excelled; and George of Trebizond, who in his last age forgot both the Greek and the Latin. And in my time Philippus Decius, an excellent Jurisconsult, was so overwhelmed with old age in the year 1536 (at which time he honoured me with the degree of Doctorate at Siena) that he did not remember any law or paragraph of our law: nay, he could scarcely utter a little word of Latin, so that when he wished to give me the insignia of the degree, and to say these three words — both the place and the time demand that we dispatch the matter in few words (*Et locus, & tempus postulant, ut paucis rem absolvamus*) — which was the beginning of his oration, he stood almost half a quarter of an hour, so that it was necessary that another doctor of the college take up the speech. But this rustic here, du Tilh, of whom we treat, was neither foolish, nor old, nor sick.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOTAT. XXXV. on p055, correctly; the running head of p056 is printed "ARREST DV." with a point, alone among the versos. The alphabet restarts at this annotation: one note, *a*, with its marker. The Latin tag is prose, not verse, and is kept in the text in parentheses after its English; Coras calls the whole clause "these three words". Wrong sorts and slips translated by sense: *caractaires* for *caracteres*; *ſçanoit* for *ſçauoit*; *vieillieſſe* / *viellieſſe* for *vieilleſſe*; *qu'ou* without grave; the print's *du Thil* for *du Tilh* is kept as a variant of the name. *Ramage* (literally birdsong; here one's own tongue) is glossed in brackets; *les noms des elemens* → "the names of the letters" (the *elementa* are the letters of the alphabet); *hebeſté* → "dull-witted"; *caduques ans* → "decaying years"; *meſcongnoiſtre* → "fail to recognise"; "[it made]" supplies the verb carried over from *firent oublier*. The exempla: Amphistides (Aristotle in Athenaeus, *Deipnosophists* 4, and the Suda: could not count past five, and did not know whether his father or his mother had borne him); Atticus, son of Herodes Atticus (Philostratus, *Lives of the Sophists* 2.1, the boy who could not learn the alphabet); Messala Corvinus (Pliny, *Natural History* 7.24); Francesco Barbaro (1390–1454) and George of Trebizond (1395–c. 1486), Italian humanists who lost their Greek in old age; Philippus Decius (Filippo Decio, 1454–1535/6), professor at Siena at the end of his life. Coras's own statement that Decius conferred his doctorate at Siena in 1536 conflicts with the case file (§3), which gives Padua, 1535 — see the report.

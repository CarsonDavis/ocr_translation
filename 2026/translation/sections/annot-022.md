⟦p040⟧This act alone made (like a true touchstone) great proof of the honesty of the said de Rols, who wou⟦p041⟧ld never ask for a separation of marriage by reason of the spell in which her husband had been held since the marriage, nine years or thereabouts; although by the law of the Pontiffs, immediately after three years, it was lawful for her to require the separation {a}. To understand which the better, it must be known that the impediment in persons to being able to cohabit carnally with their mate proceeds either from coldness, or from bewitchment, or else from some other kind of impotence {b}, which is a general term by which is signified every want of power to have to do with one's mate, whether by nature or by accident {c}. Natural impotence sometimes proceeds only from low age, {d} and this kind is common to the man and to the woman alike; who, while they are pupils (*pupilles*), that is to say the male under fourteen years and the female under twelve, have not the power (according to the common virtues of nature) to perform the act of the flesh {e}, and so are not fit to contract marriage {f}; the more so as (as has been amply shown above {g}) they have not yet sense, nor settled judgement, to give consent {h}. And although every kind of crime, such as theft, sacrilege, murder, perjury and the like, may fall in them, nevertheless that of the flesh commonly does not fall there {i}; and that is why they cannot be accused of adultery {k}. Sometimes impotence of nature proceeds from coldness {l}, that is to say from a temperament so cold that it could not be warmed, nor accommodate itself to the carnal company of a woman {m}, whoever she may be: for that man is hindered by coldness from knowing a woman who is hindered also from approaching all the others {n}. And this kind of impotence belongs to men only: a sufficient cause to prevent a marriage that is in treaty to be agreed and consummated, or else to dissolve one that should already be contracted {o}, or rather to declare it null, inasmuch as in truth such a ⟦p042⟧marriage would never have been worth anything. Natural impotence may also come from being castrated by nature, or born without testicles (I prefer here to flay the Latin [*eschorcher le Latin*: to force the Latin word into French] rather than to express this word more clearly in unseemly words), or else from having the conduit and passage of the seed naturally so twisted that the seed, constrained to halt in this detour, cannot follow, run down, nor be carried to the necessary places. {q} This impotence of nature may also be proper and particular to the woman, when she should lack the natural instrument, or be in her secret parts so tight and narrow, or otherwise hindered, that she could not suffer the carnal company of a man {r}; in which case, if there is no remedy to open the passage, it is certain that the marriage contracted could be dissolved {ſ}, or, to speak better, declared to have been in no wise and invalidly contracted {t}. Accidental impotence is either forced and violent, or secret and hidden. Forced impotence comes from the sword and the knife, when by it the member of the man is found cut off {u}; and although some also do it voluntarily, nevertheless nature remains forced {x}. Whereupon some allege, rather ill to the purpose as it seems to me, Saint Matthew, when he says that there are some eunuchs who are so born from their mothers' womb, and others who have been made eunuchs by men, and some who have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven {y}. For the Evangelist (whose design is other than the aim of our discourse) means that those who are eunuchs by nature, or by the force of men, if they are chaste and continent, ought not to carry off great praise for it, inasmuch as their chastity proceeds more from constraint and necessity than from honest affection and Christian virtue; but those who have made themselves eunuchs, not by sword or knife (for that is a detestable and accursed thing), but for the honour of God, reverence for His Gospel, and ardent desire to obey His commandments, render themselves victorious over the ardour and concupiscence of the flesh, quenching the fire and the flame which continually burns the flesh: these certainly ⟦p043⟧are worthy of great praise. But taking up again our track and the path of our discourse: the men who have thus their shameful parts cut off, because they are deprived not only of the generative power, but also of being able to approach a woman {z}, cannot in any way contract marriage {a}. Secret and hidden impotence is that which proceeds from bewitchment and sorcery {b}, by which a man is sometimes rendered impotent toward all women, one excepted (she, perhaps, who has given him the morsel or worked the spell), sometimes toward one only, and able to have to do with all the others {c}: a thing certainly little credible, if by innumerable experiences we had not some certainty of it. Now such an enchantment is not perpetual, because it can be removed, either by an interval of time, or by a contrary bewitchment, or even (and very often in our Gascony) by him who has made and ordained it {d}; for, as the Philosophers say, in human acts everything that can be bound can be unbound and dissolved {e}. Whence it must undoubtedly be believed that such an impediment does not suffice to undo a marriage already contracted {f}, unless it be after three years from the day of the nuptials, from which time, if the marriage has not been able to be consummated by carnal work, the law presumes the impediment to be perpetual {g}; true it is that some have further doubted whether this impotence was a sufficient cause to dissolve the marriage, seeing that God permits separation only for lechery and adultery {h}. A great and true reason, where the marriage should once have been consummated by carnal cohabitation {i}; but before the consummation it is without difficulty lawful for the wife (whose husband has not the power of a man) to leave him and take another {k}. There are nevertheless found some so well born, modest and honest, and who so well confine the dissolute affections of the flesh, that when any lascivious desire attempts to make a breach in their continence and modesty, it is at once extinguished and deadened; and so much so that several have remained three, four, five, nay eight and nine years (like ⟦p044⟧this de ROLS here) without making any show of complaining of the little duty that the husband rendered her in approaching her. Whereupon, not long ago, I learned from a man worthy of belief that, shortly before I wrote these things, there was separated, by judgment of the Official of Albi [the bishop's judge], the marriage of a husband who had remained eighteen years without ever having been able to approach his wife, who, being examined, was found still a virgin; and nevertheless she had never made any show of being vexed or complaining of it.

⟦p002⟧Marriages thus contracted before the lawful age ordained by nature, or by the civil laws, cannot be (if it is permissible to sound the secret and inscrutable judgments of the divinity) pleasing or agreeable to God: and the issue of them is most often pitiful and wretched, and (as one sees daily by example) full of a thousand repentances: inasmuch as in such precocious and premature unions, those who have plotted and planned the whole have in no way respected the honour and glory of God, and still less the end for which this holy and venerable estate of Marriage was instituted by Him from the beginning of the world {a} (which was, before the offence of our first Father, to fill the earth, to increase, multiply and preserve the human race by the generation of children and of posterity; {b} and after the stumbling of Adam, to avoid the lecheries and dissolutions into which many, destitute of company, were constrained to throw themselves {c}); but on the contrary, their whole aim and design has fixed upon some ambition, private profit, or other worldly vanity, by which, however, a thing so grave, so holy and so honourable as Marriage does not deserve to be soiled or contaminated in any way. I leave aside that a youth so tender, so flighty and so frolicsome cannot well be held within bounds by judgment or discretion, so as to consent {d} to an act of such greatness and importance: without which, however, everyone knows well that so venerable a union cannot lawfully be contracted {e}. And so far is it from being the case that men before fourteen years feel their virility, or that women before twelve are apt to conceive, that at that age, so delicate, neither the one nor the other can well be stirred by any goad of incontinence {f}: nor indeed even until the sixteenth year, if we believe the Egyptians, who in their Hieroglyphs, to signify sensual pleasure, were wont to carve and paint the number sixteen: inasmuch as at that age young boys begin to feel the kindlings of the flesh catch fire at the delights of the world, and sometimes to give too long a rein to their disordered affections. I well foresee here that there will be set against me I know not what old and common examples of certain children ⟦p003⟧in whose first youth the desire of the flesh once boiled up so much that one, at about nine years, got his wet-nurse with child {g}; and another, having scarcely reached his tenth, provoked by the immodest and lascivious acts of his mistress (who made him lie with her), knew her at last and made her pregnant {h}. And again of a little girl who, at about nine years, bore a child {i}. Which also, or very nearly, several attribute to the kings Solomon and Ahaz, who (according to the reckoning that some make) had children between ten and eleven years of age {k}: for Solomon had Rehoboam, and Ahaz had Hezekiah {l}. But what of it? Although for the one child (Saint Jerome, for greater assurance, calls the Lord to witness that he does not lie) THE LORD BEING MY WITNESS, he says, I DO NOT LIE (*Domino teste, non mentior*) {m}, and the other authors are likewise men of great learning and commendable good faith: nevertheless their authority is not necessary, nor so venerable, that the reader should be obliged to believe them {n}. And as for Solomon and Ahaz, many very learned writers reckon their years otherwise. Be that as it may, even if it were so, these are fine examples, certainly, and memorable: but so rare, nonetheless, that we neither ought nor can make a precedent of them {o}. And our laws in this matter, as in all others, fix upon the cases and affairs which they see or think most often to happen {p}. Indeed, that great oracle of Philosophy, Plato, speaking of the age suitable for men and women to marry, prescribed for men the thirtieth year, up to the thirty-fifth: and for women, from the sixteenth up to the twentieth {q}; and Aristotle his disciple, the thirty-sixth for males, and the eighteenth for females {r}. True it is that in this our legislators have, far more prudently as it seems to me, foreseen and considered that many are so ill-born, and of a concupiscence so immoderately forward, that if by means of the nuptial union they could not extinguish the ardour that begins to inflame and prick them, they might brutally throw themselves into filthy and detestable lusts. For which reason they have made the use of marriage somewhat earlier, and freer, than Plato or Aristotle: namely, in the fourteenth year for the man, and in the twelfth for the wo⟦p004⟧man {ſ}. Especially since at that age one sometimes sees it happen that the generative power begins to stir in the man, and the purgation of the flowers [the menses] in women {t}: and thus that the man and the woman can produce seed strong enough to conceive and beget {u}; although at the sixteenth year, and better still at the eighteenth, the natural power is far more robust and vigorous: whence our Jurisconsults have called the eighteenth year full and perfect puberty {x}, and have moreover taught that within the seventeenth year a man is still almost in his childhood {y}. Which the ancient Romans showed well in the children of Senators, or of other illustrious and honourable houses, whom until the seventeenth year they made wear the same fashion of clothing that they had worn in their first childhood, namely a long robe down to the heels, bordered all around with purple, which they called PRAETEXTA, with a small jewel of gold, in the shape of a heart, hung about their neck, which they called BULLA: so that, looking upon it, they might have occasion to think that they would not be esteemed men except insofar as their heart should be honest, good and virtuous {z}. And if anyone is still so curious as to wish to search out and understand the cause why women are sooner fit for marriage than men, I shall answer with Aristotle that, as females in their mother's womb, by reason of their coldness and weakness, are longer delayed in building and perfecting themselves, so as sometimes to wait until the tenth month — whereas the males, more vigorous and robust, scarcely pass the ninth — so also, when women are born, for the same reason of their feebleness and weakness, they grow and grow old sooner than males {a}. Whence this must be attributed to nature, which makes women sooner fit to beget, as being frailer, and sooner grown and aged {b}: after the example of every fruit, which, the smaller and slighter it is, ripens the more promptly and with the greater speed.

⟦p111⟧Arnauld du Tilh had substituted upon himself the name and person of Martin Guerre, and so subtly laid so many snares to bait and keep everyone in the persuasion of such an imposture, that the said de Rols, his wife, the sisters, the uncle and the kinsfolk of the same Martin were lulled in it for three years and more: a notable substitution certainly, if ever one was discovered, and worthy of atrocious, cruel and exemplary punishment {a}, although of this crime our laws and canons have spoken so sparingly, and so rarely, that there is found no text which can properly belong to this case. It is true that Modestinus, speaking of him who, not being a soldier, nevertheless calls and maintains himself to be such, or who uses forbidden insignia and arms, or indeed forges false letters of the Prince, wills and ordains that he be most grievously punished {b}. Pope Clement III also makes mention of a priest who had taken the name and title of a King's son, and under this cloak taken up arms and stirred up a great sedition: of the penalty he says not a single word {c}. Mention is also made of I know not what Barbarius Philippus, a slave, who presented himself to the Roman people as a free person, and as such carried off the dignity of Praetor; but whether he did well or ill, the Jurisconsult does not open his mouth with a single word {d}. Everyone knows also that mention is made in our law of the substitution of children {e}. But what! these are all crimes diverse and separate from this one; and it would certainly not be easy to give certain rules upon the penalty: as much because our laws have determined nothing about it, as also because the ancients have ⟦p112⟧taken such kinds of substitutions sometimes as a game, and have left the deed wholly unpunished. At other times they have punished it, but very gently. Others, more severe, have punished it with civil death, and some (very few in number, however) with natural death. And so that it may not seem to the reader that I have spoken on credit, I have been willing to seek out a little further afield the more noble, illustrious and memorable examples. When Jacob, to defraud Esau his elder brother of the paternal blessing, substituted upon himself, by the counsel of Rebecca his mother, the name and person of Esau, wrapping his hands and neck in goatskins so as to show himself hairy as his brother was, and dressing himself in Esau's most precious garments, he carried off by this fraud (says Scripture) the blessing of his father Isaac, and was made lord over his brothers and plentiful in goods, to whom the peoples did reverence {f}; and thus, says St Augustine, so far was he from being reproved or punished by God for it, that he received for it reward and recompense {g}. Likewise also, when Laban, having promised Rachel his younger daughter to Jacob, who had served him seven years to have Rachel, on the day of the wedding substituted in the nuptial bed Leah his elder daughter, and made her lie with Jacob, and moreover constrained him to serve another seven years to recover Rachel, God was not angered at it {h}. Laodice, wife of King Antiochus, after having killed her husband, substituted in the royal bed Artemon, feigning him to be her husband (because he wholly resembled Antiochus), so that from there he might speak to the people and commend to them his wife and his children; whereupon the people, persuaded that this commendation proceeded from Antiochus (to whom the people was devoted and most affectionate), would not afterwards elect any King without the opinion and counsel of Laodice: who by means of this cruel imposture received reward and recompense, so far was she from feeling any penalty whatever {i}. When Barbarius Philippus, the slave of whom we spoke a little before, having fled from his master, gave the Roman people to understand that he was a free man and freeborn, and under this substitution was created Praetor of Rome, neither the law nor the magistrate punished him for it: but approved and ⟦p113⟧upheld all his acts {k}, although the Emperor Augustus commanded that such kinds of slaves, so rash as to thrust themselves by like substitutions into dignities, be punished with fitting penalties {l}. The Pontiff Clement III, speaking of a seditious priest who, after having falsely usurped the name and title of a King's son, and stirred up the people to civil wars, was condemned first to the whip, and afterwards to be hanged on a gibbet, where he was executed, calls into doubt whether those who put him to death are excommunicated {m}. Of which I should not have doubted, if the substitution alone had deserved death. One reads further that Trebellius Calca substituted upon himself the name and person of the son of Clodius, to ravish and take possession of his goods, and that the fraud could not be so finely covered but that in the end the light of truth brought it to evidence, whereby he lost his cause: but that he was punished for such fraud and substitution, history says nothing of it {n}. So of the Milanese woman who called herself Rubria, in order to occupy and invade the goods of the true Rubria, deceased: one reads indeed that she succumbed through the prudence of Octavian Augustus; but that she was punished, there is no author who speaks of it {o}. I am not ignorant either that others have been punished for such substitutions, doubtful enough however. Herophilus, a physician, who called himself the son of the younger Marius, acquired so much favour and grace with the people, for the memory of Caius Marius, his pretended grandfather, who had been seven times consul at Rome, that several companies of the old soldiers, and of the towns, followed him as their ancient patron and protector: nay, when Caesar had returned victorious from Spain against the children of Pompey, those who came to congratulate him on his victory did no less honour to Herophilus than to Caesar, whose trophies and monuments were nevertheless already engraved in every corner of the earth: whereat Caesar, indignant, and fearing some sedition of the people, only banished him from Rome. True it is that after the death of Caesar, because he returned to Rome and threatened the Senate, he was by command of the Senators made prisoner, and in the house [the prison, in Valerius Maximus] executed to death {p}. Moreover, we re⟦p114⟧ad that Nicomedes, King of Bithynia, after the death of Ariarathes, King of Cappadocia, invaded his kingdom, and promised marriage to Laodice, widow of the said Ariarathes; whereat Mithridates, brother of Laodice, being angered, drove Nicomedes out of Cappadocia, and restored it to another Ariarathes, son of Laodice: whom nevertheless he afterwards had killed by one named Gordius; whereupon Nicomedes, fearing that Mithridates, having made himself stronger by the accession of Cappadocia, might afterwards invade Bithynia, suborned a young man of very good grace to present himself to the Roman people as another son of Ariarathes, and to ask of them the kingdom of Cappadocia: and so that the thing might be made more probable, sent with him Laodice his wife (who also, as we have said above, had formerly been married to the first Ariarathes) to testify that this was her child, and that of Ariarathes her first husband: but to prevent his desire from producing its effect, Mithridates sent the above-named Gordius (who by Mithridates' command had killed the first Ariarathes) to assure the Roman people of the contrary: who, having discovered the fraud and rashness of the one and the other, only deprived Mithridates of Cappadocia and Nicomedes of Paphlagonia, and gave liberty to both peoples. Nevertheless the Cappadocians refused it, saying it was not possible for any people to live without a King. Whereupon the Senate constituted Ariobarzanes their King {q}. A Jew of Sidon resembled so well in body, face, speech and bearing Alexander, son of Herod Antipas, king of the Jews (whom the father had had killed), that he gave several to understand that he was Alexander, son of Herod, giving several tokens of Alexander, and of the private matters of the house of Herod, instructed by someone who was lending him a hand. And the better to colour the imposture, he said that the soldiers who had taken charge to kill him had given him a way to save himself, and had killed another in his place: in which he knew so well how to practise, and to impose upon the greater part of the Jews, that they followed him as king, and he came to Rome in royal ⟦p115⟧state, to ask of the Emperor Augustus his share of the kingdom, where a solemn entry was made for him by the Jews who at that time resided at Rome, who carried him in a chair through the streets and crossroads, after the Jewish fashion, and showed him as a miracle. But Augustus, who naturally abhorred such kinds of trickery and substitution, suspecting that there was some eel under the rock, in order to find out the truth of the matter drew him apart and interrogated him so subtly, and struck so close to the hammer of his conscience, that in the end he drew the worms from his nose [wormed the truth out of him] and made him frankly confess the imposture, and who had induced him to do it: nevertheless Caesar was not so severe against him as the greatness of the crime deserved, but condemned him only to the galleys. True it is that he who had woven the web and so well instructed the Sidonian was condemned to death and executed {r}. I know well also that several, for like substitutions, have suffered natural death: as Smerdis, or according to Trogus Pompeius, Oropastes, who, being wholly like Smerdis (others called him Mergis), brother of Cambyses king of the Assyrians, whom the same Cambyses, frightened by a dream he had had, and fearing to be driven by him from his kingdom, had had slain by Prexaspes, easily persuaded everyone that he was Smerdis, son of Cyrus and brother of Cambyses; which was made still more probable, inasmuch as Prexaspes afterwards asserted that he had not killed Smerdis, although he had been commanded to, but out of compassion had saved his life. Whereupon in the end Smerdis, or if you prefer Oropastes, was created king, and revered as such for the space of seven months; but in the eighth he was discovered by Phaedima, one of the royal concubines, who (warned by Otanes her father), lying with Smerdis as he was asleep, and handling his head, found that he had his ears cut off (an execution formerly done upon him, for a certain misdeed, by command of Cyrus); which being learned, seven of the principal men of the country, indignant beyond measure, of a generosity and virtue to be commended, having sworn his death with great oaths, carrying their swords under their robes, went ⟦p116⟧to kill him in the royal palace {ſ}. The like disaster befell Prompalus, who, being suborned by Ptolemy King of Egypt, Attalus King of Asia, Ariarathes King of Cappadocia and the people of Antioch to name and call himself Alexander, son of King Antiochus, and as such to demand the paternal kingdom from Demetrius, who had occupied Syria, undertook it, and fortune was so favourable to him that he vanquished him in the end, and killed Demetrius, and possessed peaceably the kingdom of Syria. True it is that afterwards the sweetness of this sceptre, the affluence of so many goods and honours, accompanied by a licence and liberty unchecked, corrupted him, and so captivated him to every kind of pleasure and lechery, that the very subjects of Antioch, by whom he had been made king, seeing him plunged into this abyss and confused labyrinth of vices, revolted, and gave themselves to the son of the late Demetrius, also called Demetrius, and afterwards Nicator: who thereupon vanquished this fine Prompalus, bastard king, and drove him into Arabia, where he was slain {t}. Archelaus likewise feigned to be the son of King Mithridates, which he so well persuaded Ptolemy king of Egypt that he gave him his daughter, and moreover afterwards made him his successor in the kingdom of Egypt: but in the end Gabinius vanquished him in the field of battle and killed him {u}. A like imposture was formerly practised, in the time of the Emperor Otho, by a harper calling himself Nero, because he took after him in the lineaments and features of the face, and in the height and bulk of the body: adding that when the rumour was spread at Rome that Nero had killed himself, another had been slain in his place: which was the cause that under the name of Nero he assembled several seditious men, with whom he was going into Syria and Egypt; whence, by the disfavour of the winds, he was carried to the island of Cythnus, among the Cyclades, where he used authority over the soldiers who came from the East, and constrained them to obey him: which being learned, almost all Asia was tottering, until Otho sent two galleys, by which the one in which this false Nero was was fought and vanquished, and this new Nero killed and his body sent to Rome {x}. In the annals of France one reads that in the year one thousand 225, Baldwin, count of Flanders and fi⟦p117⟧rst Emperor of Constantinople, having been killed by the Greeks in battle (where nevertheless it was never possible to find the body, whereby many thought that he was still alive), a little afterwards a pilgrim presented himself in Flanders who resembled the late count Baldwin so well, and moreover had I know not what natural charm which won the hearts of everyone, even so that he gave such good and true tokens, that the greater part constantly maintained that he was the true count Baldwin, and as such several towns received him. But Jeanne, the count's daughter (who commanded at that time in Flanders as heiress of her father), would never recognise him as father, nor receive him as count, and asked counsel, aid and succour of King Louis VIII her uncle; who, curious to learn the truth more closely, summoned him to come to Péronne, where the king at the first meeting was greatly astonished, seeing him wholly like the late count; but remembering that Philip Augustus his father had conferred the order upon the same count, he approached him more closely, asking him the day, the place, and how he had been made knight of the order, and where he had first betrothed his wife. Whereupon this false Baldwin, seeing himself caught, asked for a delay to answer, which was granted him; and by that (and a little afterwards his fraud being still better discovered) he was found in a tavern, and a little afterwards hanged {y}. Further, I am not ignorant that there have been some in the past who, at the discovery of the deed or a little before, being overtaken by death, have escaped the cruelty of the penalties which Justice would justly have prepared for them: as Joan the Englishwoman, who, dressed as a man and conducted to Athens by a scholar who kept her, profited so much in letters, especially the sacred, that being back in Rome she found no equal, whether in learnedly interpreting and lecturing, or in subtly disputing; whereupon, accompanied by an infinity of graces with which a prodigal heaven had favoured her, and esteemed by all men, she gained so much opinion and authority with the people, singularly with the greatest, that after the death of Pope Leo III she was elected by the consent of all the Romans ⟦p118⟧to the Papal dignity, which she held two years, one month and four days: and would have held it longer, had she not too immodestly abandoned herself to a servant; by whose doings being pregnant, as she was going one day to St John Lateran, pressed by the pains, she gave birth in the middle of the street, between the Colosseum and St Clement's. Whence since then the Roman Pontiff, in horror and detestation of so monstrous and abominable a deed, when he goes to the said Saint John's, turns aside from this street so as not to pass through it. And moreover, so as not to fall into a like error, as soon as the pope is created, he is placed on the seat of St Peter (pierced for that purpose), where the youngest of the Cardinal Deacons handles his genitories from beneath, and afterwards cries aloud, The Pope has testicles (*Papa testiculos habet*) {z}. These are the principal histories of the substitution of persons that I have been able to gather: by which, nevertheless, nor by our laws either, we cannot well determine a certain penalty for this deed. Whether it be by the law of Antoninus {a}, which wills that the crime of Forgery (of which nevertheless the ordinary penalty is only banishment and confiscation — which is by Justinian taken away, and the goods preserved to the heirs {c}) when there is substitution of persons be punished capitally {d}: and although the interpretation of this word, Capitally, which may be referred to civil and to natural death {e}, must be committed to the discretion of the Judge; who, weighing all the circumstances, will consider whether the deed deserves that the accused be put to death, either naturally or civilly {f}: nevertheless in the case of our Arnauld du Tilh there are so many capital crimes assembled, each worthy of the last punishment, that there is no great reason to doubt further about it: as by what we shall say hereafter will appear more clearly.

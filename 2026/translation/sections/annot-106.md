⟦p149⟧Everyone persuaded himself, and with great reason, as I have shown above {a}, that this prodigious imposture was aided by Necromancy, or some other reprobate art: inasmuch as it was impossible by nature to say so many things, and to know persons never before seen, reciting to them the conversations which they and the said Martin Guerre had had ten, fifteen and twenty years before; together with uncovering the most particular and private acts that can pass between two married people, and that had truly taken place between the said Martin Guerre and Bertrande de Rols, husband and wife — without the help of some magic and diabolical art. Whence I cannot yet put off this opinion, though this fine rustic denied it at the execution. Especially when I remember that never did he falter in any interrogation whatever that was put to him by me, or by Monsieur the President, in full chamber, save in this one alone: namely, when the said lord President asked him (as if assured of it) whence he had the familiar spirit of which he had made use in this affair, and where it was that he had learned the diabolical invocations. For then, all affrighted, he lowered his face, and knew not what to answer: making true what Ovid says in some place {b},

Alas, how hard it is not to betray a crime by the face! (*Heu, quàm difficile eſt crimen non prodere vultu!*)

⟦p150⟧O how ill-easy it is that the crime should not manifest and discover itself in the face! And as to his now denying it: besides that this cannot alter nor change the truth of the matter {c}, there is no one who does not know that to what an accused man says (even were he at the point of death) to charge another, or to discharge himself, no great faith is given {d}. For neither can the assertion of one man harm another {e} — as Moses himself had written in his laws {f} — nor can anyone (though he be esteemed a great man of worth, and placed in honour and great authority {g}) bear witness in his own cause {h}. Although, to the profit of another, the assertion of a third party may sometimes avail: as for example, the declaration made by one who is about to die of some wound, the author of which is uncertain, tending to the discharge of another who is accused of having struck the blow, is of such force, and so profits the accused, that he cannot afterwards be put to the torture, even though otherwise the indications were sufficient {i}.

⟦p008⟧Here is a new kind of effrontery and impudence: not, however, very unlike the argument of Plautus in his first comedy, where he brings in Jupiter extremely in love with Alcmena, wife of Amphitryon, and, having no hope of being able to overcome her chastity by presents, entreaties, or other allurements of love, and knowing that Amphitryon had gone off against the Teleboans, Jupiter takes the form of Amphitryon and, feigning one night to have returned from the war, abused Alcmena: who, being already with child by her husband's doing, was once again got with child by Jupiter; whence it afterwards came about that she, at one and the same delivery, was brought to bed of two sons, the one Amphitryon's, called Iphiclus, and the other Jupiter's, who was named Hercules. Whereupon the finest part of the fable was that, Amphitryon being back, and having sent ahead Sosia, his servant, to his wife, to announce his coming, Sosia finds Mercury (who had already taken the form of Sosia, being likewise Jupiter's servant), and they dispute at length between themselves which of the two was the true Sosia. But at last Mercury, victorious, drives the other off: and hardly less befell Amphitryon, who is roughly received by his wife, persuaded that he was the impostor and wished to abuse her. At last Blepharo is chosen arbiter, to judge which of the two is the true Amphitryon, and he, for the entire likeness that there was between them, could never distinguish the one from the other; whereat Amphitryon, more astonished still, wished to have recourse to the Diviners. Which Jupiter seeing, to show the innocence of Alcmena, discloses the whole matter at length to Amphitryon, and restores him to peace and friendship with his wife {a}.

## Notes
- {a} (p008): **Plautus, *Amphitryon*** — Plaute en ſon Amphytrio. [Coras's "first comedy": *Amphitruo* stands first in the alphabetical order of the plays. Coras's *Iphyclus* is Iphicles, the twin of Hercules, whom Plautus does not name; the arbitration by Blepharo and Jupiter's disclosure belong to the play's fragmentary fourth act and the fifth.]

⟦p124⟧To show clearly that this impostor du Tilh was attainted and convicted also of sacrilege, it must be understood that although of old the Gentiles and Pagans (who placed all their religion and hope, together with the greatness and majesty of their gods, in idols) thought that alone to be sacrilege which was stolen from the temples dedicated to the honour and service of their idols; and although some time afterwards, occasion being taken from this, sacrilege came to be properly referred to theft committed in Churches, or of sacred things {a}; nevertheless, by a larger interpretation, those who despised the gods were of old also cal⟦p125⟧led Sacrilegious. Thus Ovid, in some place, calls Lycurgus Sacrilegious, because he had despised the sacrifices of the god Bacchus. In the same way the Christians have generally called sacrilege every pollution and profanation of a sacred thing, every contempt and irreverence toward God, or toward the things instituted by him {b}: as for example, an abuse committed upon the sacrament of Baptism is rightly called Sacrilege {c}; so too a violence done to the Ministers of the Church {d}. Likewise a piece of magic, inasmuch as the magician abuses holy words in the invocation of spirits {e}. Thus he who traffics and makes merchandise of spiritual things is with good reason said to be Sacrilegious {f}; nay, he who disputes the power of the Prince, and calls into doubt whether the man whom he has chosen and called to his service is sufficient and worthy, deserves the title of Sacrilegious {g}. We read also that Sallust called Terentia, Cicero's wife, Sacrilegious {h}, because she was so troublesome, and so depraved in her manners, that Cicero, on his return from exile, was constrained to repudiate her {i}. Wherefore there is no doubt that those who despise, abuse and profane a thing so holy and sacred as marriage deserve to be called and judged Sacrilegious, and as such are worthy of death {k}, the ordinary penalty of the sacrilegious, who have been at all times so odious that the ancients at times burned them alive {l}. And although pity and compassion for age, in other crimes and even in this one, ought to incline the judges to some gentleness and moderation of penalty {m}, nevertheless Aelian relates of a young child who of old had taken a gold plate from the crown of the goddess Diana: because, when afterwards there were set before him dolls and other little things (in which little children delight), he chose the plate again, he was condemned by the Athenians to death {n}.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOTAT. LXXXV. on p124. From here the print's numbers run one ahead of the true count (it gave LXXXIII twice, to Annotations 82 and 83): this is the 84th annotation, and it expounds the word *Sacrilege* of the decision (texte-83). Markers a–n (no *j*) on p124–p125, all keyed and served. The page break falls inside *appel|les* and is kept at the corresponding word ("cal⟦p125⟧led"). Reflow gaps read through: *Mi niſtres*; in the margin *ſa crilegium*. Wrong sorts translated by sense: *honnenr* for *honneur*; *ces poupees* read as *des poupees*; in the margin *dailect.* for *delict.* (note *m*). The print's *(auſquelles les petis enfans ſe plaiſent il) choiſit* misplaces the pronoun inside the parenthesis; translated as *il choiſit*. Three `⟨alt⟩` readings in the margin (notes *b*, *d*, *i*), none affecting the English; see the report. Coras uses *Sacrilege* as a noun of person (*nomme Licurgus Sacrilege*, *appelez … Sacrileges*), a sense English once had but has lost; it is rendered by the substantive adjective "Sacrilegious", with Coras's capital, and the crime itself by "sacrilege". *Gentils, & Ethniques* → "Gentiles and Pagans" per the glossary (*Ethniques & Payens*); *colloquoyent* → "placed"; *contemnoyent* → "despised"; *polution, & profanation* → "pollution and profanation"; *vne magie* → "a piece of magic"; *traffique & fait marchandiſe* → "traffics and makes merchandise" (simony); *reuoque en doute* → "calls into doubt"; *faſcheuſe* → "troublesome"; *repudier* → "repudiate"; *toutesfois* in *les anciens les ont toutesfois bruſlez* is taken in its older sense "at times" (Ulpian, cited at *l*, says some were burned alive, others thrown to the beasts or hanged); "nevertheless" would make no sense after *ſi odieux, que*; *la pitié, & compaſſion de l'aage* → "pity and compassion for age" (the Digest's *miseratio aetatis*, cited at *m*); *douceur, & moderation de peine* → "gentleness and moderation of penalty"; *tablete d'or* → "gold plate" (Aelian's πέταλον, a gold leaf of the crown); *poupees* → "dolls" (Aelian has knucklebones and toys); *de rechef* → "again". Coras's "returned from exile" for Cicero's divorce of Terentia is his own statement (Plutarch places the divorce after Cicero's return from Caesar's camp after Pharsalus, not after the exile of 58–57 BC); it is translated as he gives it. The sacrilege Coras attributes to du Tilh is the profanation of marriage, by the Code's *sacrilegos nuptiarum* (note *k*).

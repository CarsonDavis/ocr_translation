⟦p102⟧Although death is the end of all evils, the rest from all misery, and a strong bulwark against the calamities of this world —

It brings to our ills rest, and to our labours an end (*Illa malis requiem finemque laboribus affert*).

And although for that reason it ought not to be dreaded by a man of worth, but rather contemned and despised {a2}; and although Numantius, writing to Marcus Cicero, says that one ought often to desire an honest death, by which a man passes beyond innumerable perils and crosses of the inconstant fortune of this miserable world — so far is it from being a thing one ought ⟦p103⟧to flee {b}; and although Saint Paul burned with a continual desire and ardent zeal to attain to the heavenly inheritance, and to be separated from the body, to dwell with Jesus Christ {c}: nevertheless, to wish at certain times, and for some vexation that befalls, for the last sigh and period of one's life, is the speech of a person too much subject to her passions, and, for the rest, ill instructed in the law of God; of whom, being his vassals and most bounden servants, we must wait upon all his commandments. And, as Hegesippus said, we must not wish to depart from this world sooner, nor to remain in it longer, than the good God who has given us being and life wills and commands it. And it is not lawful to cut the thread of life, or to dissolve the soul from the body, for any other than him who joined it. And just as Plato says that those who by authority of the magistrate are made prisoners must not go out of the prison before the magistrate by whose authority they were put in orders it, so neither must we, without the will of the Lord God who has given us the soul, drive it from us, draw it out, or put it forth from the prison of this mortal and miserable body {c2}. Saint Augustine in a certain place occupies and labours himself greatly to show the great fault committed by those who desire, and still worse those who execute, a voluntary death upon their own persons, singularly when they do it for fear of punishment or of infamy {d}. Which before him Aristotle had learnedly discoursed, saying that it is not the act of a constant and virtuous man, but rather of a timid and cowardly one, to slay oneself with one's own hands, and to make oneself the cruel minister of one's own death, in order to flee punishment, poverty, or some other discontent {e}. I know well that in time past Hannibal, Cato, Cassius, Brutus, Nero, Diocletian, Sardanapalus, Cleopatra, and several others, so as not to fall into the hands of their enemies, killed themselves, or had themselves killed by their servants. And others have done no less from an impatience of grief, wearied by the martyrdom of some sad and lamentable passion {f}: as the Emperor Hadrian, and Silius Italicus, an excellent poet, both overcome by incredible torments of sickness; as ⟦p104⟧also Lucretia and Porcia, Roman women — the one too much grieved by the outrage received from King Tarquin, who had raped her; the other greatly troubled at hearing of the death of Brutus her husband. And that others too, weary of dealing in the traffic of this world, have executed the same, to unburden the spirit of this mortal load and to set it at rest, in the beatitude they hoped for — as the Gymnosophists and Brahmans in the Indies; as Cleombrotus, the Ambraciot Philosopher, who, after having read Plato's *Phaedo* on the immortality of the soul, written in the person of Socrates, to seek a surer rest threw himself headlong from a high wall into the sea, so as the sooner to gain the company of the eternally happy {g}; of whom Ovid, speaking, said {h}:

Or may you come into Tartarus from a steep rock, like him who read the Socratic work on death.

And Callimachus, a Greek poet, made upon him an elegant epigram, since rendered into Latin by some learned man, as follows:

"Life, farewell," you said as you died, Ambraciot youth, fallen headlong from the high wall. There is no evil in death; but the writings of learned Plato were not thus to be taken by your mind.

Others, for an ostentation and vain hope of eternising their memory, and to build themselves some trophy of honour in the ages to come, as Cleanthes, Chrysippus and Zeno in Greece {i}; the Decii and the Curtii at Rome, Menoeceus at Thebes, Codrus King in Athens.

Codrus, not afraid to die for his country (*Codrus pro patria non timidus mori*) {k}.

And several others, among whom Empedocles, that great Philosopher, who, to get himself esteemed a God, being near Mount Etna in Sicily, which ever burns, stole away from his companions and secretly threw himself into the fire, with the intention that, not appearing afterwards, he should be put among the number of the immortal gods; of whom Horace {l}: ⟦p105⟧I shall tell it, and I shall recount the death of the Sicilian poet: Empedocles, while he longs to be held an immortal God, coldly leapt into burning Etna. Let it be right, and let poets be allowed to perish.

Nevertheless he was well frustrated of his hope: for the violence of the fire cast out the little slippers of tin which he used to wear, which uncovered all his ambition and imposture. Now, to come back to our sheep [*reuenans à nos moutons*: to our subject]: it is a very vile thing, cowardly, unworthy of a Christian, and most displeasing to God, to wish to forestall one's days, to slaughter and undo oneself with one's own hands before the Lord calls us; from whom we have in keeping the soul he has given us. And if you have taken into your keeping money, or rings, or other things of your friend, and misuse them, you are with very good reason esteemed wicked, disloyal and perverse: with greater cause, therefore, if you abuse, cast out and drive from you so precious a thing as is the soul, which God has committed to you and delivered into your keeping.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOTAT. LXXVII. on p102; the transcription numbers it 76 (the printed numbering runs one ahead, flagged uncertain). The body carries the markers {a2}, {b}, {c}, {c2}, {d}, {e}, {f}, {g}, {h}, {i}, {k}, {l}, each served by a keyed note: {a2} on p102 (the first note is printed *a*, and is keyed *a2* because p102 already carries Annotation LXXVI's note *a*), {b}–{f} on p103 (the fourth note is printed *c* like the third, a wrong sort where the run wants *d*; the transcription keys it *c2*, and the marker is given so here, the following notes resuming at *d*), {g}–{l} on p104. The page breaks fall between *la* and *doiue fuir* ("ought ⟦p103⟧to flee"), inside *com|me* ("as ⟦p104⟧also"), and between *Horace {l},* and the Latin *Dicam*, and are kept at the corresponding words. Reflow gaps read through: *Nu mantius*, *quel que*, *de uant*, *cho ſe*; *ſurmõtez* and *ſouuẽt* expand as usual. Wrong sorts and slips translated by sense: *conioinre* for *conjointe*, *ſortit* for *ſortir*, *moius* for *moins*, *fachez* for *faſchez*, *léu* for *leu*, *detain* for *d'eſtain*, *inſque* (margin) for *iuſque*. Three `⟨alt⟩` markers fall in the body and four in the margin; the choices are in the batch report. The print's lower-case sentence-breaks after *ſes paſſions* and *infamie* and its full stops inside running sentences (*en tous ſes commandemens. & cõme diſoit…*; *de maladie, com|me auſſi*) are regularised. The long opening period — *Bien que la mort ſoit… & que Numantius… die… & que ſainct Paul… toutesfois ſouhaitter… eſt parole d'vne perſonne* — is an anacoluthon in the French: the clause on Saint Paul has no finite verb (*bruſlant d'vn deſir…*); the English supplies "burned" and keeps the three "although" clauses leading to one "nevertheless". *Ses paſſions* is rendered "her passions", the person meant being Bertrande. The verses: the single hexameter *Illa malis…* and Horace's *Codrus pro patria…* are translated in the text with the Latin in parentheses; the couplets and the four lines of Horace are translated in the text and the Latin given in the Notes below, each verse paragraph of the print kept as a paragraph. Horace's lines run on from *of whom Horace {l}:* in the print without a paragraph break and are kept so.

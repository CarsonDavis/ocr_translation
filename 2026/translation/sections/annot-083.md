⟦p118⟧There is no need to explain more clearly the adultery of which the said du Tilh remained sufficiently, and more than sufficiently, at⟦p119⟧tainted and convicted: but only to speak of the penalty, in which the old Romans do not show themselves severe, inasmuch as in those times there were no lurkers after other men's marriages, and one did not hear tell of immodesties nor lecheries {a}; which was the reason also for which Lycurgus in Lacedaemon established no penalties at all against adulterers {b}. As to our Jurisconsults: it seems to many that they did not make the penalty of this crime capital {c}, nay, that they did not wish to punish it with a simple banishment, unless when the adultery was joined with incest, as if one had abused a married kinswoman of one's own {d}. Nevertheless our Emperors, especially the Christian and Catholic ones, after the example of the Law of God (by which adulterers must die {e}), have holily judged this crime not only capital, but, passing yet further, worthy of the sword and of natural death {f}. Among whom Opilius Macrinus, the twenty-third Emperor, had the two bodies of the adulterous man and woman tied together and burnt alive together {g}; nay, one day he had two of his soldiers (who had violated a woman) put into the bellies of two oxen, each into his own, and their bodies there sewn up and enclosed entirely, except the head, which showed, so that they might be seen speaking together and deploring their misery {h}. But yet, weighing well the texts of our Jurisconsults, whatever may have been thought until now, they have done no less; which our Justinian shows expressly, when he says that the Julian law on adulterers (interpreted by the Jurisconsults in the Pandects) punished with the sword, that is to say with death, those who thus profane and violate marriages {i}. It is true that for the quality of the persons, or other circumstances, sometimes this penalty of the sword and of natural death has been restrained and moderated to banishment or other civil death {k}. As also in like terms we read of the Cornelian law, written against murderers, by which homicides are punished with death {l}; and nevertheless, by reason of circumstances which sometimes present themselves, a lighter ⟦p120⟧penalty is imposed, namely banishment {m}. And those who have leafed through our books of Law with some judgment are not ignorant that the penalty of the sword may be taken in two senses, Naturally and Civilly. The natural penalty of the sword cuts and makes dissection of members {n}, and most often separation of body and soul {o}. The civil one is imposed more lightly (by reason, as I have said, of the circumstances), in banishment, and galleys for a certain time or perpetually {p}; nay, if we speak according to the constitutions of the Roman Pontiffs, in excommunication and ecclesiastical censures only {q}. And although the law of God punished both the adulterous man and the adulterous woman with death {r}, nevertheless our emperors, as to the woman, ordain otherwise: whom they have willed to be chastised, and afterwards put into a monastery, whence the husband has the faculty, within two years, of recovering her; which having passed (if the husband takes no account of her), she is bound to take the habit of that convent, to remain there and bewail her sin perpetually {ſ}. On the contrary, Romulus, desiring greater chastity and continence in women {t}, punished them with death, and left the men unpunished {u}. Whence afterwards was drawn the usage that the husband might kill with impunity his wife found in adultery, and nevertheless the wife dared not so much as touch with the tip of her finger the husband caught in a like fault — which did not contain in itself (said Cato holily) right, reason, nor rectitude of justice {x}. But what! if we wished to punish adulterers according to the manners or laws of the Gentiles, certainly we should find ourselves greatly confused: for some have chastised them in one way, others in another: some punished severely, as the Parthians, Egyptians, Locrians and Arabs; others gently, as the Lepreans, Gortynians and Pisidians; and some have left this crime wholly unpunished, as the Indians, Massagetae and certain other nations, among whom one may well place the Nomads, who have always wished to have their wives in common among them {y}. And our fine Canonists do not deserve to be forgotten, who under the banner of the Pontiff Alexander III seem to have put adultery ⟦p121⟧in the number and catalogue of the smallest and least crimes {z}. And although St Clement, successor of St Peter (or, according to others, fourth Bishop of Rome), had taught them that after heresy there was no offence more horrible and displeasing to God, nor which deserved to be more sharply and rigorously punished {a}: nevertheless they, having put this crime in the number of the light ones, have willed that for adultery a clerk cannot be degraded, or actually stripped of his sacred orders {b}; for this penalty, they say, is peculiarly reserved for great, enormous and execrable crimes {c}. But in what rank shall we be able to put our Frenchmen? who (if the testimony of Jean Faure and Guillaume Benedicti is believed) long ago put adultery in the number of ingenious and highly praised acts: so far were they from repressing or punishing it {d}. Which, by the frequent and multiplied judgments of our company, we have long since made everyone touch with the finger and know to be false, and to have been by them too inconsiderately, and with no little scandal, written and asserted. For if it were so, as they write (which however I could not persuade myself), that for some time lechery had been so far winked at in France that, instead of punishing it and holding it in horror, and especially adultery, some praise was given to it: what more certain argument could we have for esteeming that the judges of that age deserved the title of Barbarians, or of Turks (licensed by their law to every dissoluteness), rather than of Christians? and that they had no light of the knowledge of God, nor of his word? by which we are first taught that our members are members of Jesus Christ, which must not be made members of a harlot, nor soiled by lechery, nor any passion of carnal concupiscence, but possessed in honour and sanctification {e}. And afterwards, that lechers and adulterers have always been severely pursued by God, not only by the old law, which condemned them to death {f} irremissibly, and, as Saint Gregory expounds, without any mercy {g}; but also by the new ⟦p122⟧law, which admonishes us not to deceive ourselves: for lechers and adulterers, says St Paul, shall not possess the kingdom of heaven {h}, and shall be judged by the living God {i}. Therefore, if some by misfortune had been so asleep until now as to connive at such crimes, it would be high time for them to awake from so deep a sleep, and, unsealing their eyes, to consider not so much what heretofore might have been done, whether here or elsewhere, as what is ordained and commanded us by God, or established by the civil laws {k}: especially when the adultery is aggravated, as in this case here, where the adultery is found qualified by a monstrous, and before this day unheard-of, treachery. One must not therefore doubt that the said du Tilh, by this crime alone, deserved death {l}: for, for a much lesser deed, a tavern servant, having abused his mistress, made drunk and asleep in the bed of her absent husband, was, by a decision of the Parlement of Paris pronounced in May 1551, hanged and strangled {m}. I do not know, however, whether from this penalty of death ordained against adulterers one could justly exempt, if not wholly (at least in part), priests, monks and others who by their own vow have voluntarily enslaved and bound themselves to perpetual continence. Which several have believed; for, they say, such kinds of people (to all of whom the Lord God has not dispensed the grace of being able to contain themselves), if they burn in such concupiscence, have no place where they may honestly satisfy their thirsty and carnal pleasure. Therefore, if they give themselves to some woman, even though she be married, they seem little or not at all to deserve some excuse [unclear: *ſemblẽt peu, ou point meriter quelque excuſe*; the argument being reported requires the opposite sense, that they deserve some excuse, and a word may have dropped] — like him who, constrained by hunger, steals a little food to appease it {n}. But this opinion is full of impiety, and the reason very cold: inasmuch as, in the first place, in things commanded or forbidden by the law of God, nay even by nature, simplicity, necessity or temptation does not excuse him who contravenes {o}. Otherwise, a poor woman who went astray to relieve her misery would deserve to be excused: a thing too iniquitous to think, and which the Gentiles themselves have detested {p}; for man must ra⟦p123⟧ther endure, and patiently suffer all the calamities of the world, and were it death, before consenting to the least thing evil and forbidden by his Lord God {q}. Moreover, it is greatly to be imputed to the priest or monk who, trusting too much in himself, and not recognising the frailty of his flesh, has too easily and rashly thrust himself forward to make such a vow. Thus, if he stumbles and does not keep it, but soils his body by adultery, so far is he from deserving to be excused from the penalty, that he is in my opinion more reprehensible and punishable than another, as having failed more grievously and doubly: namely by rashness, and by contravention and disobedience to the commandment of God, who forbids all pollution and lechery, singularly adultery {r}, which he wills to be (as we have already often said) punished with death {ſ}; unless the circumstances should lighten the penalty somewhat, or remit it altogether: as ignorance might well do {t}, force {u}, the tenderness of a young age, drawn on by continual lascivious and immodest acts {x}. And so of other like cases, left to the discretion of a good, holy and equitable judge {y}.

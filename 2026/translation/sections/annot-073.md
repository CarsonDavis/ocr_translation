⟦p097⟧Since Pliny, Plutarch, Valerius and other historiographers testify to us that several men and wo⟦p098⟧men of old died of a sudden and excessive joy, it will not, in my opinion, be found new that a person weeps from great joy, and sheds tears in abundance — witness Ptolemy King of Egypt, who, when he was presented with the laws of Judaea written in letters of gold, began from an extreme joy to lament and weep {a}: for, as Josephus says in relating this history, nature oftentimes, for a sovereign pleasure, suffers that which most often befalls those who are sorely grieving and sorrowful {b}. Whereupon one might adduce infinite fine examples, related by grave authors worthy of credit, of those who, surprised by an unmeasured joy, have not only wept, but have even died suddenly on the spot {c}; of whom I shall take only Diagoras of Rhodes, who, seeing his three children on one same day crowned as victors in the art of wrestling well, and taking their crowns to set them upon his head while kissing him — and the people afterwards, rejoicing with him, throwing flowers at him from all sides — from an incomparable joy gave up his soul between the arms of his children {d}. I leave aside the Poet Philemon, who, seeing an ass eat the figs that had been prepared for the dinner, began to laugh so vehemently that he died of it on the hour

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOT. LXXIII. on p097. The transcription numbers it 72 and flags the number uncertain: from here the printed numbering runs one ahead of the count of annotations actually set, and the file ids follow the count, not the print. The body carries the markers {a}–{d}, all on p098, each served by a keyed note. The page break falls inside *fem|mes* and is kept at the corresponding word ("wo⟦p098⟧men"). The body ends at *ſur l'heure* with no point in the print, and none is supplied. Reflow gaps read through: *hiſtorio graphes*, *Rho dien*, *couron nez*. The print's *ſouffrez* (for *ſouffre*, "suffers") is translated by sense; *de grand ioye* (for *grande*) likewise. *Hiſtoriographes* → "historiographers"; *iadis* → "of old"; *ſoudaine & exceſſiue ioye* → "sudden and excessive joy"; *à mon aduis* → "in my opinion"; *iette larmes en abondãce* → "sheds tears in abundance"; *Ptolemee Roy d'Egypte* → "Ptolemy King of Egypt" (Ptolemy II Philadelphus, as at annot-030, where the same anecdote is told with the same citation); *les loix de Iudee* → "the laws of Judaea" (the Law, sent from Jerusalem for the Septuagint translation); *ſouuerain plaiſir* → "sovereign pleasure"; *bien dolens & marriz* → "sorely grieving and sorrowful"; *graues autheurs & dignes de foy* → "grave authors worthy of credit"; *deſmeſuree ioye* → "unmeasured joy"; *morts ſoudainemẽt ſur la place* → "died suddenly on the spot"; *l'art de bien luitter* → "the art of wrestling well"; *rendit l'ame* → "gave up his soul"; *ſur l'heure* → "on the hour". The Philemon anecdote (the ass eating the figs, the fatal laughter) is in Valerius Maximus 9.12 ext. 6 and Lucian, *Macrobii* 25 — Valerius being one of the authors Coras names in his first sentence; the print gives no marginal note for it. This annotation picks up the physiology of tears of joy from Annotation XXX (annot-030), to which note {b} sends the reader.

[The same passage is cited at annot-030 {a2}.]
- {b} (p098): **Coras, Annotation XXX (cross-reference; annot-030, on why both the grieving and the joyful weep)** — Soit veuë l'annotation xxx.
- {c} (p098): **Pliny, *Natural History* VII, chapter 4 as printed — the deaths from sudden joy (Chilon of Sparta, Sophocles, Dionysius of Sicily, Diagoras of Rhodes) are at 7.53 (7.53.180–181), chapter 53 in the old division, so the print's *iiij* has probably lost an *l*; and Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* III.15 (deaths from unexpected joy: Polycrita of Naxos, the comic poet Philippides, Diagoras of Rhodes)** — Pline au li. vij. c. iiij. Aul. Gelle au liure iij. c. xv. [Gellius 3.15.3 is Coras's immediate source for Diagoras: three sons victorious at Olympia on one day, the crowns set on their father's head, the people throwing flowers, and the father dying in their embrace.]
- {d} (p098): **Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* I (1.111)** — Ciceron au j. des Tuſcul. [Diagoras of Rhodes, told by a Spartan, "Die, Diagoras, for you will not go up to heaven" (*Morere, Diagora, non enim in caelum ascensurus es*); Cicero gives two sons, Gellius three — Coras follows Gellius.]

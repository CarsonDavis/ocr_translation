⟦p126⟧As for the pillage and theft, it is evident in this case, not only of the property, part of which this venerable impostor devoured and sold to one and another, but also of the honour of the said Martin and de Rols. I shall be told that theft is not a capital crime, still less worthy of death. Which, by the usage of the Lacedaemonians and Egyptians (who leave all thefts unpunished), would be indubitable {a2}; nay, even according to our laws, I confess it: for Justinian himself has protested that he does not wish anyone to die for theft, nor to have any limb cut off, but that he be otherwise punished at the discretion of the Judge {b2}. Nevertheless this is to be understood of simple thefts, which no legislator whatsoever (Draco alone excepted) has found worthy of death, but only punishable in money, at double, triple, fourfold or fivefold {c2}, or else by some light correction {d2}; because they reckoned (as I believe) that thefts, or the most part of them, were committed more through want and necessity than with ⟦p127⟧the intention of enriching oneself, or of harming one's neighbour {e}. I have excepted only Draco, the ancient legislator of Athens, who by his cruel and bloody laws put all criminals to death indifferently, and singularly thieves, even supposing they had stolen but a mere pennyworth of herbs {f}. Nevertheless, inasmuch as he appointed one and the same penalty for all misdeeds, however small they were, even to punishing with death a little idleness and sloth, all these laws, which Demades used to say had been written in blood and not in ink, were, as barbarous and inhuman, removed and abolished, or at the least, by a tacit consent of the Athenians, consigned to oblivion; one alone excepted, by which murderers were condemned to death {g}. But thefts which are atrocious, and qualified by some notable gravity, must also be more grievously and severely punished {h}, and sometimes even to death, whether civil or natural {i}: as are the sacrilegious, despoilers, *diaetarii* [chamber-thieves], and breakers of the peace {k}. Among all of whom our rustic du Tilh will readily find a place. For in the first place he is sacrilegious, as has been shown above {l}. Next, he is a despoiler, having left nothing of Martin Guerre's property unstolen {m}. Further, he is a *diaetarius*, or as others read, a *directarius*, having taken possession by stealth and by treachery not only of a chamber, to rob it, but of the whole house {n}. He is moreover a breaker of the peace, and so punishable with death, were the theft but of five *sols* {o} — the more so in this case, where it is a question of having troubled a peaceable and well-accorded marriage, which is a violation and great rupture of a commendable public peace and tranquillity {p}, and which the laws have ordained to be grievously and severely punished, even though no act of lechery should have followed from it {q}.

⟦p152⟧From this may arise a fine and noble question: whether credit ought to be given to the assertion of the said du Tilh, made at the hour of his end, and whether sufficient proof may be gathered from it of what he confessed to owe, or affirmed to be owed to him. On which the most certain and common resolution is that the assertion, saying or declaration of one who is about to die, although it makes proof to his own prejudice, or to that of his heir {a}, is nevertheless invalid and of no effect whatever to the disadvantage of another {b}. And indeed, as it is reasonable that each should be believed in what he attests against himself {c}, so it would be beyond reason that credit should be given him to the prejudice of another {d}; yes, even though it were the father or the mother who, in their last days, attested something against their own ⟦p153⟧child {e}. Whence, for our purpose, it would be an almost ridiculous thing, of pernicious and very bad example, that a man could make a debtor at his will, or otherwise prejudice him {f}. And that is why our Jurisconsults will not have credit given to one who is dying of a wound and charges another with having wounded him, if there is no proof from elsewhere {g}; although I would not deny that such a delation gives rise to some slight presumption against the one whom the dead man accuses {h}. For although, says Baldus, every man who is about to die is not St John the Evangelist, nevertheless it is not likely that he should be so wholly forgetful and so little mindful of his salvation, even at the extremity of his life {k}, as to wish to denounce another falsely {l}. I have said expressly "some slight presumption": for to say, with some, that the sole assertion of the murdered man, charging the accused, is sufficient indication for torture {m}, seems to me (I speak always under the censure of the more learned) to have neither sense nor appearance of reason. For besides that this manifestly contradicts and is repugnant to the words and reason of the laws which have spoken of it {n}, if it were true the wounded man would be a witness in his own cause, against the common decisions {o}; and nevertheless his testimony would be worth two, inasmuch as two witnesses are required to prove an indication for torture {p}; which would be, in one and the same matter, to introduce two special things, and far too irregular {q}. For the same reasons we say that if a witness, Judge or Notary, at the last breath of his life, confessed to having borne false witness {r}, pronounced a judgment {ſ}, or forged a false instrument {t}, for money or some other kind of corruption, credit would nonetheless not be given them to the prejudice of the party to whom the right was already acquired {u}. For the confession of one who is about to die cannot harm or prejudice another. Besides which there is a great presumption to the contrary: that the witness, Judge or Notary who has delayed until the article of his death to make such a confession has made it falsely, at the suggestion and subornation of some⟦p154⟧one who kept at his side for these ends during his sickness {x}. Added to which, the attestation of this kind of people, who by their own confession are perjured and infamous {y}, ought not to be received, except insofar as, by the circumstances of the business, the judge might be moved to give them some credit and belief {z}. On which subject Marcus Cicero somewhere says that when someone has once perjured himself, he must not afterwards be believed, nor any credit given him, even though he should swear by several gods {a}. Thus then, to return to our track: if one who, by testament or other disposition made at the hour when he thinks to die, confesses to owe Jean or Pierre a hundred *écus* by friendly loan or by deposit, this confession, in truth, inasmuch as it is made in the absence of the party, cannot bring him loss as long as he lives {b}, but he may freely and without any difficulty whatever retract it {c}. True it is that if he does not expressly revoke it, that suffices after his death to compel the heir to render to the said Jean or Pierre the said hundred *écus* confessed {d} (singularly if such declaration or confession was made for the discharge of conscience {e}); yes, even though such confession were made by the husband in favour of his wife {f} — between whom, however, and the husband, gifts, as everyone knows, are not tolerated nor received {g}. Accursius says further, in some other place, that if the testator confesses to have received from Jean or Pierre a hundred *écus* by friendly loan, the heir from whom Jean or Pierre demand them cannot oppose to them the exception which we call "of money not counted out" [*non numeratae pecuniae*], inasmuch as there is no occasion whatever to think that one who thinks to die on the instant, or shortly after, has made such a confession in the hope that the said sum of a hundred *écus* would afterwards be counted out and paid to him {h}. And if anyone here wished to say that such a confession, although made by one who thinks to die, does not prove the debt, even were that confession confirmed by oaths {i}, he [Accursius] answers and confesses that from such a declaration sufficient proof cannot be gathered ⟦p155⟧to make it evident that such an obligation was contracted. Nevertheless it has the effect and force of a bequest: that is to say, the heir may be compelled to pay the said hundred *écus*, if not as owed, at least as left by legacy or fideicommissum {k}. Except in two cases. The first, when it should appear that such confession had been made by error and imprudently {l}; for then there is no consent or will of the disposer {m}, inasmuch as, as Ulpian says, there is nothing so contrary to consent as the error which clearly discovers the imprudence and simplicity of the speaker {n}. The second, when such confession should be made in favour of a person to whom the law forbids, or does not permit, that anything, or very little, be left {o}; because it is then to be presumed, and very likely, that such acknowledgment was made to defraud the law and its intention {p}, which by this means could always be frustrated and annulled. As (by way of example) if someone wished to leave to his second wife more than is permitted him by the law of Justinian, that is to say, more than to one of the children of the first marriage {q}, and in his testament confessed to have received from his said wife a thousand *écus*: such confession, although the testator husband should assure by a thousand oaths that it was true, would profit nothing {r}. And as to saying that the confession of a debt, made by the testator and fortified by oath, makes sufficient proof {ſ}: that is understood where deceit and fraud are absent; for the religion of the oath gives no authority to a wicked and fraudulent act {t}. Now when the confession is made in favour of a person incapable by law, the fraud is patent {u}. Nor is it anything to say that a gift made between married persons (such as the said acknowledgment may be called), although invalid, is nevertheless confirmed not only by the silence and death of the donor {x}, but also by oath. For the difference is very great between the oath which is interposed upon a gift and that which intervenes upon an acknowledgment: inasmuch as the former intervenes upon what is to come — for he ⟦p156⟧who presently gives something swears to keep his liberality and not to contravene what he promises and gives; but he who acknowledges and swears to have long since received some sum of money or other thing interposes his oath upon what is already done and past, that is to say, upon what has already before been received by the acknowledger.

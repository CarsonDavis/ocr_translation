⟦p156⟧A fine question presents itself here: whether this institution of an heir is valid, and so whether a man condemned to death can make a testament. And although such a dispute may seem to many to be without difficulty, because our Jurisconsults, and with them Justinian, teach in divers places that testaments already made are quashed and broken by the condemnation to death that follows — so far is it from the condemned man's being able to make new ones after the sentence {a} — nevertheless grave authors in our Jurisprudence relate that they have often seen testaments made by those condemned to death, but have never heard of the quashing of any {b}. Added to which, by the new constitutions of Justinian it seems that this rigour of the law, refusing to permit the condemned the faculty of making a testament, has received some change {c}, because the reason on which the interdiction of making a testament, in those condemned to death, was founded (which is that where someone was condemned to death his goods also were confiscated, and so, having no goods, he could not make a testament {e}) seems to cease today, the goods being, by the new law, exempt from confiscation and reserved to the nearest successors ab intestato, except ⟦p157⟧in the crime of lese-majesty {f}; whence, if the fisc is excluded by the heirs ab intestato, with all the more reason it is by the testamentary heirs, who altogether extinguish the force and virtue of intestate succession {g}. It is true, to say frankly {h} how matters stand, that these allegations are more acute and subtle than true. For as to what the interpreters of Italy say, that they have never seen the testaments of those condemned to death put in difficulty: they themselves also confess that by the law such dispositions are invalid {i}. And it may be, too, that they were called into doubt in the very time of those who have so written; nevertheless that did not come to their knowledge. And touching the second reason, it must be considered that the confiscation of goods is not the sole cause for which the faculty of making a testament is forbidden to those condemned to death; but there is another, and more principal: because he who is condemned to death is made a slave of the penalty {k}, and diminished in his head {l} — that is to say, he has lost his liberty, his citizenship and his family all together; or else, retaining liberty, has lost his citizenship, as we still see today in those who are confined. And so [they are] incapable of making a testament {m}; the more so if we confess, with the greater part of the Interpreters, that the testament is of the civil law: the slave can do nothing, nay, is esteemed as a dead thing {n}. Someone will perhaps be obstinate here {o} with Justinian, saying that he who is well born — that is to say, who is not in servitude, but of free and freeborn persons — cannot by any penalty of punishment whatever be made a slave {p}. To whom it will promptly be answered that this new constitution (as Accursius and the others commonly interpret {q}) is to be understood of those condemned to civil death, or to some other penalty short of natural death, and so of those who survive the execution of the penalty to which they are condemned, and not at all of those who are condemned to suffer natural death, that is to say the separation of body and soul {r}. True it is that this interpretation has always seemed to me to do too much violence to these general words of Justinian, who in ⟦p158⟧both places speaks very generally of all the well-born, and nevertheless speaks indefinitely of punishment {ſ}; and he who speaks universally of all comprehends all also, and excludes nothing {t}; and a proposition indefinitely pronounced weighs as much, and is of as great force, as the universal {u}. But what! In France we are outside this dispute, inasmuch as by the general custom of our Gaul the goods of one who is condemned to death — not only for the privileged crimes of lese-majesty, heresy or false coining, but also for every other crime from which there follows natural death, mutilation of a limb or perpetual banishment — are confiscated, and the new constitution of Justinian has no place {x}; so that, as by a perpetual law, we say in France: HE WHO confiscates the body confiscates the goods {y}. And so the condemned man, being deprived of all his goods, could not make a testament {z}. By the first reason touched on above, one single case is admitted in which he who is condemned may make a testament: namely, when he is condemned by a judge not competent over him, as for example, and following the laws of our Pontiffs, if a clerk were condemned by a lay judge {a}; in which case the condemned man does not lose the faculty of being able to make a testament, or otherwise to dispose {b}, inasmuch as all the procedure done by the judge is without any force, and as if not done {c}. And where there is want of jurisdiction and of power, the judgment never draws to itself the effect of the law {d}.

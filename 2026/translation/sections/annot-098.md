⟦p138⟧Greater indeed was the difficulty with regard to the said Bertrande de Rols, for several considerations. The first, for the too great facility which she used in receiving so imprudently this cheat du Tilh for her husband, and having believed him too lightly {a}; moreover having conversed, drunk, eaten and slept with him for the space of three years under the cloak of such an error, which she approved well enough by not contradicting it {b}. Added to which also (which will serve for the second reason), during these three years she often heard murmuring, and several gave her intimations of it — nay, expressly told her that this personage was not Martin Guerre; against whom (although she had reason to believe them, or at least to doubt) she nevertheless fought vigorously, asserting the contrary — in which she seemed manifestly to disclose her fault. For just as a possessor is called a possessor in good faith until such time as he knows, or doubts, whether the parcel he has acquired belongs to another than him who delivered it to him {c}, so a woman who lies with another than her husband is excused so long as she is ignorant of it and thinks she has to do with her consort and spouse; but as soon as she comes to know the contrary, or indeed to suspect it, and nevertheless has to do with him and suffers herself to be known by him, she is inexcusable {d}: for from the moment she begins to perceive or suspect the fraud, she ought at once to separate herself from him {e}. In the third place, taking a case not too dissimilar: Lot, after having drunk well, got with child his two daughters, who had secretly lain down in ⟦p139⟧his bed — he thinking, nevertheless, that he had to do with his wife {f}; and yet St Augustine, although he excuses the incest, holds him guilty nonetheless of having let himself be vanquished and overcome by wine {g}. The last reason: that although there was no fraud, deceit or evil intention on the part of the said de Rols, the act is nevertheless so prodigious and evil, and the adultery of such pernicious example, that it ought to be punished in her {h}. Which is nothing new in our law — namely, that a person without fault be punished if there is some great cause or public reason which commands it {i}. Of which the example is at hand, of a curé, rector or prelate, if he becomes a leper: for because of the danger, scandal and abomination of the people, he will indeed be deprived of the administration of the benefice and of the Church {k}. And if someone has married a widow, or indeed a virgin who afterwards has fornicated, he is grievously wronged, so far is he from being at fault; and nevertheless this fault and lechery of his wife punishes him, and hinders him from being promoted to holy orders or admitted to any ecclesiastical ministry {l}. But on the contrary, for the excuse of the said de Rols, there comes first into consideration the weakness of her sex, easy to be deceived by the cunning, craftiness and sharp practice of men {m}, and in which the law does not readily presume fraud or any intention of doing wrong {n}. In the second place, the error in which she was justly placed, by reason of the great likeness there was between the said du Tilh and Martin Guerre, accompanied by innumerable tokens which this du Tilh had given her, of the most private, together with the sisters and uncle of the said Martin — nay, to all those of the place of Artigat who presented themselves to him, and who on this account had all received him as Martin Guerre — ought to excuse the said de Rols, after the example of Lot {o}, of whom we have spoken, and more properly still of Abimelech, King of Gerar, who had taken possession of Sarah, Abraham's wife, and wished to abuse her, thinking she was Abraham's sister, as he had assured him; nevertheless he was excused and pardoned by God, because Abimelech had undertaken this deed by error and, as the Scripture speaks, in the simplicity of his heart and the purity of his hands {p}. And indeed in a like ⟦p140⟧case our Pontiffs excuse the husband beside whom, as he lies in bed, his wife's sister comes to lie down, if he has to do with her thinking he has to do with his wife {q}. And if a woman marries the husband of another, believing however that he is not married, and they lie together, the woman is excused {r}. And Jacob was not reproved for having had to do with Leah, Laban's elder daughter, because he thought he had to do with Rachel, who was promised to him {ſ}. Thirdly, an error, even though its source has no good foundation, excuses the person who errs, and makes him presumed to be exempt from all evil purpose and from all fraud {t} — and even though the cause of the error were unjust and bad, nay inept, foolish and rash {u}. If then in the said de Rols there is no evil intention, it follows necessarily that there is no adultery on her side, which cannot be committed without the purpose and will to fornicate {x}. Fourthly, in the matter of crimes, he who takes one person for another, having no will to offend anyone, is excused {y}: as for example, if thinking to chastise my servant (which is permitted me {z}) I strike a free man, I am excused, and cannot be sued in an action of injuries {a}. I have said expressly, without intention of doing wrong, for otherwise the error would not excuse him: as if I had purposed to kill Antoine and, thinking to slay him, I killed Pierre, this error would not excuse me from being held as a murderer and homicide {b}. For although I did not wish to do harm to Pierre, yet my principal intention was to kill a man {c}. In the fifth place, the little fault that could be here, if there is any, would rather be imputable to Martin Guerre, for having remained so long absent, than to the said de Rols his wife {d}, for what has been said above after St Augustine {e}. For the last: the Emperor Constantine, in a like case, seems to have determined this matter. For, the case being put to him of a woman who, after having remained four years without news of her husband, who had gone to the war, sends to learn from his captain whether he is alive or dead, and afterwards publicly remarries, he answers that she is exempt from all punishment ⟦p141⟧and beyond all suspicion {f}. And briefly to resolve all the contrary arguments, it must be considered that the said de Rols, having been persuaded, together with the sisters and the other principal kinsfolk of the said Martin, both by reason of the likeness and of the tokens, that the said du Tilh was truly her husband — she, having her conscience thus informed {g}, was not bound to give faith or credit to those who said the contrary, especially persons who had no interest in it {h}; considering singularly the danger in which she was putting herself, the shame she was uncovering of her own deed, and the peril of receiving a greater one if the denunciation had been found calumnious and false {i}. Which is a reason that comes greatly into consideration, and by which Papinian excuses the woman violated by force, if she has had shame to disclose such a deed at once to her husband {k}; and the Emperor commands that the reputation of a woman of worth and honour be entirely preserved to her {l}. By the above there is answered also what is imputed to the said de Rols, of having believed too lightly that the said du Tilh was her husband {m}. For she did not believe lightly, considering the interval of time of eight years and the absence of the said Martin, the tokens given by this du Tilh, the assurance that the sisters and uncle of the said Martin gave her, the error and opinion of the rest of the inhabitants of Artigat, who had received, held and esteemed him Martin Guerre, and the longing she had to see and recover her husband. Added to which, as St Ambrose says, a man of worth believes easily {n}, and this facility proceeds only from a goodness and praiseworthy simplicity {o}; and to punish here the said de Rols without fault {p} there is no sufficient cause, nor any scandal or bad example, seeing her error and the just reasons she had to adhere to it. The argument from the history of Lot {q} is of no service here, for Lot was never judged guilty of having had to do with his daughters, inasmuch as he thought he was approaching his wife; but he was only reproved for his drunkenness, which gave the daughters the boldness to approach their father and converse carnally with him {r}.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOT. XCVIII. on p138; the transcription numbers it 97. It expounds the words *Bertrande de Rols* of the clause of dismissal (texte-96), and with annot-096 is the annotation on which Davis's reading of Bertrande rests (case file §10): Coras sets out four reasons for condemning her and six for excusing her, and the reader is meant to see both. Where he states her innocence the statement is translated; where he grants that she "seemed manifestly to disclose her fault" that is translated too; nothing is smoothed.

⟦p029⟧This defence had some verisimilitude: for, as Jesus son of Sirach said, THERE IS nothing more iniquitous in the world than to love money and to covet the goods of another, and nothing more wicked than the avaricious man {a}, who, insatiable, is never sated nor satisfied, and to heap up riches and enlarge his fortune finds nothing ⟦p030⟧evil or impossible {b}, though he must shed, overturn and destroy the blood of half of mankind, nay of all his kinsmen. Of which Pygmalion, King of Tyre, will bear certain witness, who treacherously killed Sichaeus, his first cousin and the husband of Dido his sister, to make booty of his treasure and his goods {c}; Polymnestor also, King of Thrace, who by great treachery killed Polydorus, son of Priam (given into his keeping at the time of the Trojan war), to seize his gold and his silver {d}. And what is still more marvellous, Eriphyle, too covetous, spurred by I know not what goad to obtain the rich jewel that Adrastus, King of the Argives, had, dared to undertake to betray and disclose Amphiaraus her husband, who had hidden himself so as not to go to the war of Thebes, for fear of being killed there as had been foretold to him {e}. Whereupon the Poet cried out well, saying {f},

To what do you not drive mortal hearts, accursed hunger for gold?

That is why the Emperor Marcus Antoninus, a prince generous and excellent in every virtue, dreaded nothing in his life so much as the name and repute of an avaricious man, nor ever detested anything with such great vehemence as avarice, {g} mother, source and root of all evils {h}, and which, as Sallust says in some place, overturns faith, probity and every virtue {i}.

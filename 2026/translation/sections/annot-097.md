⟦p135⟧The greatest difficulties in the judgment of this trial, and those over which the Court laboured most, were whether Martin Guerre and Bertrande de Rols were in the way of condemnation. For as to Martin Guerre, he seemed altogether inexcusable, for several reasons. The first, for having left his father, mother, wife and children indiscreetly {a2}. The second, for having remained so long ⟦p136⟧— namely twelve years and more — absent from his wife, and so for having been the cause of the disorder which has followed from it {b}, as has been copiously demonstrated above {c}; whence follows the common decision of the law, that he who gives the occasion of a misdeed renders himself guilty of the crime {d}. The third and last: that during the time of his absence he withdrew to the enemy, and served them in the business of war against our King his natural prince, falling thereby into the crime of lese-majesty {e}. Nevertheless the Court dismissed him from the case and suit, considering that what he did proceeded from no evil will, but from a heat and lightness of youth which then boiled within him — of which the proper and peculiar vice (says Seneca) is to be unable to govern or tame the fury of the impetuous assaults which the ardour of that inconsiderate age, ever inclined and prone to evil {f}, incessantly delivers to it {g}. And as prudence is the proper virtue of old age, so instability also, indiscretion and rashness are peculiar to youth {h}, covetous of changing country, of seeing new things and of leaving the present and best-loved ones, as Horace learnedly describes when he says {i}:

The beardless youth, his guardian at last removed, delights in horses and dogs and the grass of the sunny Campus; wax to be bent to vice, harsh to those who warn him, slow to provide for what is useful, prodigal of money, high-spirited and full of desires, and swift to abandon what he has loved.

And as to saying that the said Martin gave occasion to the said de Rols his wife to receive another for her husband, and so that if there was a fault it is to be imputed to him {k}: besides that this reason has been sufficiently answered above {l}, it must be considered that the occasions of doing evil are of two kinds. The one is near and neighbouring to the deed (which the Interpreters commonly call imme⟦p137⟧diate); the other is distant and separate. As to the first, we confess that he who furnishes the occasion very closely approaching the crime is himself guilty of the deed, so much so that he cannot object it against him who committed it {m} — as, to the point of our question, if the husband had lent a hand to his wife so that another might abuse her {n}. But when the occasion is very distant and separate from the crime, he who gave it is not guilty of the misdeed {o} — as, in our terms, if the husband absents himself, perhaps too long, and the wife, incontinent and disorderedly lubricious, abandons herself to another: this can charge the husband neither with adultery {p} nor with pandering, nor excuse the wife. True it is that the husband is not without fault before God {q}, for having so inconsiderately and indiscreetly left the person for whose sake he was commanded to abandon father, mother, brother, sisters and all the rest of the world {r}. And to answer the last reason, it must also be considered that in all crimes one must regard the intention and the will of him who commits them {ſ}; and singularly in crimes of lese-majesty there is need to balance and weigh carefully the circumstances, and especially the quality of the person, and whether that person has premeditated and machinated anything against his prince {t}, or else has undertaken it through indiscretion or lightness — in which cases our laws do not readily draw such a fault to punishment; of which, although such fools and overweening persons are more than worthy, Modestinus nevertheless wills that they be pardoned as madmen {u}. And the crime of lese-majesty cannot be imputed to him who has had no will to conspire against his prince or his commonwealth {x}: as this Martin Guerre, who went away as a young lad to Spain, where the Cardinal of Burgos, and after him his brother, made use of him as a lackey, and from there took him into Flanders, where, St Lawrence's day falling in the year 1555, he took up arms before Saint-Quentin against the French — more by constraint and the necessity of obeying his master (whose hands he could not escape) than from any will he had to offend his natural prince. And yet for all this he has not failed to pay his scot [*l'eſcot*: his share of the reckoning] to inconstant fortune, which has since dealt him cruel ⟦p138⟧crosses, both for having taken a leg from him and made him lose a good part of his property (which that beggarly knave du Tilh devoured and squandered), and for having presented to him on his return the horrible miseries and calamities of this prodigious deed. Wherefore to burden him further with punishment would have smacked more of the odour of some cruelty than of the integrity of justice.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOT. XCVII. on p135; the transcription numbers it 96. It expounds the words *Met hors de procez, & d'inſtance, ledit Martin Guerre* of the decision (texte-95), and is the first of the two annotations (with annot-097) that Davis's reading of the case rests on (case file §10): Coras's own statement that the greatest difficulty of the trial was whether Martin Guerre and Bertrande de Rols should be condemned. Translated as literally as English allows; the hesitations are his. Twenty-one markers on p135–p137: the first keyed *a2* by the transcription (p135 already carries annot-095's *a*), then b–x (no *j*, *v*, *w*; *ſ* as a key between *r* and *t*), all served. Page breaks: *longue|ment* (p135/p136), kept before "— namely"; *imme|diate* (p136/p137), kept inside the word; *cruel|les* (p137/p138), kept at "cruel ⟦p138⟧crosses". Eighteen `⟨alt⟩` markers, listed in the report; none changes the English. Reflow gaps read through: *au tre* (*autre*), *in continente* (*incontinente*), *ma ri* (*mari*), *qua lité* (*qualité*). Wrong sorts translated by sense: *deſordé* (*deſordre*), *volõnté*, *tellemeut* (*tellement*), *la donnee* (*l'a donnee*). *Conſpirer auec ſon prince* is translated "to conspire against his prince": the sense required by the sentence (lese-majesty is conspiracy against the prince), the print's *auec* notwithstanding. *En l'an 1555*: the print's date for St Lawrence's day at Saint-Quentin; the battle was fought on 10 August 1557 (case file §2), and texte-32 gives the day without a year; the print's figure is kept. *Beliſtre* → "rascal"; *payer l'eſcot* → "pay his scot", glossed in brackets. *Leſe maieſté* → "lese-majesty" per the glossary (annot-031); *procliue* → "prone" (annot-073); *outrecuidez* → "overweening"; *pourpenſé & machiné* → "premeditated and machinated" (cf. *malice pourpensee*, *ordir & machiner*); *maquerelage* → "pandering" (cf. *maquereau* → pander, annot-002); *tenu la main à ſa femme* → "lent a hand to his wife" (the phrase of annot-002); *bailler l'occaſion* → "furnish the occasion"; *republique* → "commonwealth"; *laquais* → "lackey"; *naturel prince* → "natural prince"; *trauerſes* → "crosses"; *deuoré & dependu* → "devoured and squandered"; *reſenti l'odeur* → "smacked … of the odour".

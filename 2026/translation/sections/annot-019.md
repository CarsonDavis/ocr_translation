⟦p035⟧It is rightly the punishment of calumniators (that is to say, of those who falsely and with premeditated malice denounce another for some crime) to suffer one like to that which the accused would have to endure, if he were attainted and convicted of the crime imputed to him {a}. A fault certainly inhuman, detestable and great, and one which has at all times been so odious to the good Emperors that they would not allow any colour of right, any abolition public or private, nor even a special permission of the prince, to be able to protect it {b2}. And our canonists have justly judged calumniators to be worthy of punishments more cruel and grievous than the accused, if they were convicted of the crimes {c2}; and the reason seems to me plain enough: for the other crimes are very often committed without intention and previous deliberation to do wrong, through some light and inconsiderate passion {d2}. As a murder, by one who is overcome by anger; a th⟦p036⟧eft, through want and necessity; a blasphemy against God, slander against the Prince, insult against one's neighbour, through a slip and looseness of the tongue: but a calumny is always deliberate, conspired and maliciously premeditated; in detestation of which the Lord God was not content to instruct us by the mouth of Moses to build no calumny against our neighbour {f}, but has moreover straitly commanded us to deliver the oppressed from the hand of the calumniator, lest his indignation go forth like fire, and, kindled by the malice of such affections, there be found afterwards none who can quench it {g}. Likewise he has given several great and proper epithets to Satan, to show his cruelty and malicious cunning; as when he has called him Serpent {h}, Dragon {i}, Asp {k}, ravening and roaring Lion {l}; but among them all he has not wished to forget the one that is most fitting to him, namely, to name him Calumniator, {m} inasmuch as he is a liar and false accuser, who strives incessantly only to throw our conscience into confusion, to make us find evil what by the grace of God we have done well; and on the contrary, exalting and magnifying our evil works, only to keep and harden us in them. There is certainly no crime in a commonwealth worthy of being punished and repressed with so great severity as a calumny: and yet in this wretched age of ours it is made a game of, and the reins are found so slackened for calumniators that it is as it were permitted them, with impunity, to conspire, contrive and machinate all kinds of ruses, wiles and wickednesses against good people. O noble saying of Domitian, who said that the prince who does not chastise calumniators lends a hand to their malice and upholds them {n}. Alexander the Great, when he presided at the judgment of capital crimes, was accustomed, to obviate calumnies, to stop one of his ears with his hand, in order to keep it whole and free from all calumny for the accused; and if he could hear, or only smell the odour of some calumny, he was inflamed with anger so bitterly that he often became cruel and inexorable.

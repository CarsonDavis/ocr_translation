⟦p037⟧If by the ordinary and common rules of law it is with great reason forbidden to sequester goods {a} — that is to say, to separate them from the hand and power of the possessor, to put them into third hands {b}, because one ought not easily, nor without urgent reasons and cognizance of cause, to deprive anyone of the possession of his property {c} — with all the more reason the sequestration and separation of persons ought to be prohibited, especially when one wishes to take them from the company of those who belong to them very nearly, such as fathers and mothers, children, or other near kinsfolk {d}. And on the contrary also, just as, when there is good and sufficient cause (such as a fear that the parties may come to arms {e}, or suspicion of flight or of poverty {f}), it is undoubtedly permitted to sequester movable property and the fruits of the immovable {g}, likewise as to persons there is no need to make difficulty that, when there is some suspicion and fear of seduction, or other legitimate cause, the Judge may justly proceed to the sequestration of a man or of a woman, and put her in the company of good people, who keep her from speaking and conversing with suspect persons {h}. As for example, if the husband demands to be restored to his wife, who nevertheless justly fears his aus⟦p038⟧terity and harshness (having perhaps had experience of it beforehand), she ought to be committed to some woman of worth, and honest, until the end of the suit {i}. And when the husband fears that the wife whom he demands may be suborned against him, the Judge ought to have her lodged and placed in a house where she fears neither force nor violence; as of old was done in a convent of nuns and religious women. {l}

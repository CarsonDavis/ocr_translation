⟦p151⟧This suit and demand for property which the said du Tilh was making against the said Carbon Barrau his uncle (inasmuch as the matter here was criminal and capital, where the witnesses and proofs must be entire, and clearer and more shining than the sun {a}) gave great colour to finding good the objection which the same du Tilh had, at the confrontations, proposed against the said Carbon his uncle. And it is nothing to say that the civil law, which has rejected the testimony of one against whom we have a suit in a criminal matter, does not for that reason reprove it if the suit is brought civilly, in a civil and pecuniary matter {b}, as was the case between the said du Tilh and Barrau. For to this there are two answers. The first: that this ruling has no place where the objection is proposed by one accused of a crime {c}, especially if the crime is capital, and so the head is at stake. For besides that, as we have said above, the proofs in criminal causes must be clean, must shine clearer than the sun {d}, and be exempt from all suspicion {e}, it is both certain and reasonable that where the peril is greater, affairs must be handled more warily and with greater circumspection {f}. The second answer: where the suit between the parties should be for all their goods, or for the greater part, the ruling above would have no place {g}; because a suit so weighty (for goods are esteemed as the second blood and the life of a man {h}) seems to nourish and produce I know not what enmity, and no small one {i}; and a great enemy cannot (where it is objected against him) be a witness against his ⟦p152⟧enemy {k}. Nay, even though the enmity should not appear, if he is ever so little suspected of it {l}. And the reason may easily be gathered from what Cicero has eloquently left in writing. Our ancestors, he says, did not wish to open this road to enmities, that it should be lawful for anyone to injure his enemy by his testimony; inasmuch as men are so far carried by passion in the hatred they bear to another, that they would readily advance and fabricate a thousand lies to injure him {m}. In which Cicero shows that he had in no wise caught the scent of the Christian Law, by which it is commanded — not, as the Scribes and Pharisees thought and taught it to be written in the old law: Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy {n} — but more holily, to love our enemies, bless them that curse us, do good to them that hate us, pray for them that calumniate and persecute us, that we may be children of our Father which is in heaven {o},

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOTAT. CVIII. on p151; the transcription numbers it 107 (the printed numbering runs one ahead, flagged uncertain). The body carries fourteen markers, {a}–{o} without *j*, all served by keyed notes; the note for {i} is printed in the margin of p152 although its marker stands on p151, and the transcription records it as an orphan on p152 (`missing_notes: i@p151`) — it is listed below under its key. The body ends on a comma in the print (*qui eſt és cieux {o},*), the sentence left hanging before the TEXTE. that follows; the comma is kept, as the house kept the open endings of texte-73 and texte-75. Two `⟨alt⟩` choices (one in the body, one in the margin of {l}), recorded in the batch report.

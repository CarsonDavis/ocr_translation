⟦p009⟧One of the most singular fruits and most precious effects of friendship is the sweetness and the pleasure one has in being able freely to disclose one's secrets and one's thoughts to one's friend, who is another self. Is there anything in the world more singular (says Cicero somewhere) than to have a man with whom you can, and dare, speak as to yourself {a}, and by whom, said Plautus, you will never be deceived {b}.

No one is deceived, in my opinion at least, who tells his affairs frankly to his friends (*Decipitur nemo, mea quidem sententia, / qui suis amicis narrat recte res suas*).

O what a great good it is (added Seneca) when hearts are so well prepared that every secret descends into them in safety: whose conscience you fear less than your own; whose talk removes and calms the disquiet of your mind, whose opinion gives you counsel, and whose sight rejoices and consoles you {c}. Certainly the happiness and good is so great that Socrates and Darius said that all the lands and goods of this world cannot be compared with a good, true and prudent friend. Alexander the Great, passing by Troy, crowned the statue of Achilles, and praised him as fortunate in nothing so much as in having had Patroclus for a friend. O happy Achilles (said he), who had in your life so loyal and entire a friend as Patroclus {d}. True it is that such and so perfect friends as an Achilles and Patroclus, a Pylades and Orestes, a Damon and Pythias, a Theseus and Pirithous, are not to be found today, so unhappy is our age.

That once venerable name of friendship is prostituted, and sits for hire like a harlot. No one is loved unless fortune favours him; ⟦p010⟧and as soon as she thunders, she puts to flight all who stood nearest.

Nay, even of those in whom one may place some trust, they are found with such difficulty that the Phoenix at times is not so rare: indeed in this time the disgraces in friendship are so great that many profess intimate friendship with us, and show themselves outwardly more than ours, whose spirit and understanding are nonetheless disloyal, full of every treachery, and on every side our adversary: and under the honest cloak of friendship are our great enemies, worse certainly, and more dangerous, than those who openly declare themselves such {f}. For what plague could one imagine more violent, pernicious, or more effective to harm, than a familiar enemy {g}, who has irreparably injured us before we suspect him? whereas we can easily avoid him who openly shows himself our adversary {h}. That is why one must be very prudent in choosing a friend, and eat a hogshead of salt with a man — that is, converse long with him — before placing one's trust in him and committing to him anything of one's most secret matters {i}. Debate your cause (said the Sage) with your friend, and do not reveal your secrets to another: lest perchance he who hears you reproach you with it, and that blame return upon you {k}. A stricter maxim is further attributed to Bias, namely that one should so love a person as to think that one might also some day hate him {l}; which Publilius the Mime-writer afterwards took over among his gravest maxims, saying:

*Ita amicum habeas, posse ut fieri inimicum putes.*

Hold your friend in such rank that you reckon he may in the future be your enemy. Words which, as Scipio writes in the works of Cicero, are the most hostile to friendship that one could devise, and so barbarous that he could not persuade himself that Bias, one of the seven sages and so renowned, had spewed them out. For how is it possible that you should be the true friend of one whose enemy you fear to be in the future {m}? It is quite true that, as there is nothing permanent in this world {n}, friendship at all times and in all persons cannot be lasting until the ⟦p011⟧last sigh of life, inasmuch as the manners and affections of men often change, whether through prosperity or through adversity, or through the heaviness of old age; and sometimes friendships part over contentions and quarrels, or over some good, profit and advantage to which each one pretends and aspires particularly for himself {o}. But that, for such a separation of friendship, people should afterwards come to disclose and reproach one another with the secret things they had previously communicated to each other — that, in my opinion, is done only among wretched and deplorable persons.

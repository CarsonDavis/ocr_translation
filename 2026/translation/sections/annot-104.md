⟦p146⟧It was truly a tragedy for this fine rustic, inasmuch as the issue of it was most deadly and miserable for him. Whereupon: no one knows the difference between tragedy and comedy. For although both are kinds of fable {a}, comedy nevertheless describes and represents, in a low and humble style, the private fortune of men — such as loves, and the ravishing of maidens — so that thereby one may learn what one ought to imitate and follow in this life, and what one ought also to avoid: whence Cicero in some place calls comedy an imitation of life, a mirror of custom, and an image of truth {b}; so named from KOMAI, ⟦p147⟧a Greek word which signifies what the Latins call PAGUS, that is to say a town and village, and ODE, which signifies song: which is as much as to say the song of the villages and villagers. For in the beginning the Greeks, when they wished to rebuke and tax the vices and evil life of certain persons, were wont to assemble through the villages and the crossroads of the towns, and there, singing, to publish the life of those whom they wished to objurgate and reprove. In this kind of fable and poetry, although the beginning was troublous and sad, the issue nevertheless was happy, pleasant and agreeable: as all the Comedies of Plautus and of Terence demonstrate. But in Tragedy there are represented, in a high and grave style, the manners, adversities and calamitous life of captains, Dukes, Kings and Princes; it having always been so called because the first prize that was proposed for the best players of this kind of poetry was a GOAT, which the Greeks call TRAGOS.

He who in tragic song competed for a paltry goat {c} (*Carmine qui tragico, vilem, certauit ob hircum*).

Or else a Goat's skin full of wine: and ODE, which signifies song — that is to say, Goat-song. And this kind of fable always has a sad, unhappy and lamentable issue: whence is now drawn and taken the manner of speaking, which many in this time use, of calling unhappy and unfortunate deeds (even though they are transacted among vile and abject persons) plays of tragedy.

## Notes
The print heads this annotation ANNOT. CIIII. on p146; the transcription numbers it 103 (the printed numbering runs one ahead, flagged uncertain). The body carries three markers, {a}–{c}, all served by keyed notes. The page break falls after *à κομαι⟨alt:KOMAI⟩* and before *vocable Grec*; the English keeps it after "KOMAI,". Two `⟨alt⟩` choices, recorded in the batch report: the word is set in Latin small capitals, KOMAI, on the page image (reading B), in the same sort as PAGUS, ODE, TRAGOS and BOUC; and Horace's *vilem* is lower-case (reading A). Coras's etymology is the ancient one: Greek κῶμαι, "villages", + ᾠδή, "song" (Aristotle, *Poetics* 3, 1448a35–38, reports the Dorians' derivation of comedy from κώμη), and τράγος, "he-goat", + ᾠδή for tragedy. *Surquoy nul ne ſçait la differẽce* is rendered literally, "Whereupon: no one knows the difference"; the sense wanted is plainly "not everyone knows", but the print says *nul* and the English keeps it. *Ce gentil ruſtre* → "this fine rustic" (*rustre* → rustic, annot-021; the case file §10 has "this fine peasant" for the same phrase, and the "double note" of condemnation and admiration it carries); *l'iſſue* → "the issue" throughout, the outcome of the play; *fort funeſte* → "most deadly"; *eſpeces de fable* → "kinds of fable" (*fabula*, a play); *pourtant* → "nevertheless", the concessive sense the syntax (*bien que… pourtant*) requires here; *la fortune priuée des hommes* → "the private fortune of men"; *rauiſſemens de pucelles* → "the ravishing of maidens"; *increper & taxer* → "rebuke and tax"; *obiurguer, & reprendre* → "objurgate and reprove"; *faſcheux* → "troublous"; *vne peau de Bouc* → "a Goat's skin", Coras's capitals kept; *ieux de tragedie* → "plays of tragedy". Horace's line is kept on its own line as the print sets it, translated with the Latin in parentheses; the Latin names are given in their English forms (Plautus, Terence, Cicero, Horace). Reflow gap read through: none.

- {a} (p146): **Cicero, *On Duties*, book 1** — Ciceron au premier liure des offices. [The passage where comedy and tragedy are treated together as kinds of poetic fiction was not located; cf. *De officiis* 1.97–98 on the poets' observance of what is fitting in each character, and 1.104 on the two kinds of jest.]
- {b} (p146): **Cicero, *For Sextus Roscius of Ameria*** — Ciceron en l'oraiſon pro Sext. Roſcio. [The definition of comedy as *imitatio vitae, speculum consuetudinis, imago veritatis* is transmitted by Donatus (*De comoedia* 5.1), who attributes it to Cicero; it is not in the extant *Pro Sexto Roscio Amerino*, whose nearest thought (16.47) is that the poets' fictions show us our own manners in other persons.]
- {c} (p147): **Horace, *Art of Poetry*, line 220** — Horace au liure de l'art poëtique. [*Carmine qui tragico vilem certavit ob hircum*: the poet who first competed in tragic verse for a paltry he-goat — the etymology of tragedy from the goat given as the prize.]

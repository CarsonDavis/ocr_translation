# Case file: Jean de Coras, *Arrest memorable du Parlement de Tholose* (Paris: Galliot du Pré, 1572)

Reference document for translators and reviewers. Everything here is a working
aid, not an argument. Where the sources disagree, or where a fact could not be
checked, it is marked `[unverified]` or flagged as contested.

---

## 1. What this book is

The full title of our edition reads, in Coras's French:

> ARREST MEMORABLE DV PARLEMENT DE TOLOSE, contenant vne Histoire prodigieuse
> d'vn supposé mary, aduenuë de nostre temps: enrichie de cent & onze belles &
> doctes Annotations. Par M. Iean de Coras, Conseiller en la Cour, & Rapporteur
> du Procès. Prononcé és Arrests generaux, le douziesme Septembre, 1560.

An *arrest* (modern *arrêt*) is the judgment of a sovereign court — a parlement
— as distinct from a *sentence*, the judgment of a lower judge. *Mémorable*
means "worth recording." The title thus means roughly "A Memorable Judgment of
the Parlement of Toulouse"; Davis renders it "Memorable Decree," Ringold and
Lewis "A memorable decision of the High Court of Toulouse," and most scholarship
simply leaves it in French. *Arrests generaux* were the solemn general sessions
at which the chambers sat together to pronounce the court's most important
decisions. The book is therefore a
published law report — but a law report written by the judge who had the case in
his hands, about a case he got wrong.

**The *rapporteur*.** An appeal did not come before the chamber as oral argument.
A single *conseiller* took the dossier as *rapporteur*, read the whole written
record — *information*, *interrogatoires*, depositions, *confrontemens* — reduced
it to a report, delivered that report to the assembled chamber and proposed a
disposition, on which the others voted. Coras's title page advertises him as
*Rapporteur du Procès*. That is his claim to authority, and it is why the
narrative reads like a dossier digest rather than a chronicle: he is printing the
report he gave the court.

**Structure.** The book alternates two kinds of block, marked by rubrics in the
original: `TEXTE.`, a paragraph or sometimes a single clause of the court record;
and `ANNOTATION I.` / `ANNOTAT. V.` … through `ANNOTAT. CXI.`, Coras's commentary
on the *Texte* block just given. Nested inside the annotations are **lettered
marginal citations** (`a`, `b`, `c`…, restarting within each annotation), keyed to
superscript letters in the text and carrying the Roman-law, canon-law and
classical references — a mix of abbreviated Latin (`l. hodie. D. de poen.`) and
French (`Pline au 7. livre de la naturelle histoire, c. 3.`). See §6.

The annotations are unevenly distributed. Long stretches of narrative pass with
one short note; then a single word of the verdict — *faulseté*, *rapt*,
*sacrilege*, *estre pendu & estranglé* — draws several pages. The last part of the
book, Coras's exposition of the words of the verdict, takes the *arrest* apart
term by term.

Five sixteenth-century editions in all. The **1561 first edition carried 100
annotations**; the **1565 added eleven**, and its title page says exactly what
they are for: they were "newly added **on the record of the execution** of the
said decision" — the confession and hanging of 16 September 1560. The printer's
address to the reader in the 1572 edition says the addition was made "at the
continual supplication and prayers of his well-wishers." Readers wanted to know
what happened after the sentence was read.

Two facts about our copy-text are worth stating plainly in the front matter.
**The 1572 Paris edition is the one Natalie Zemon Davis used** — all her
annotation numbers and page references in the 1988 exchange are to this printing
— **and it is the edition behind the Ringold and Lewis translation**, whose
header reads "printed in Paris, with royal permission, 1572." Our references,
Davis's and Ringold's therefore line up without conversion. Finlay, by contrast,
worked from the 1561 text, which is why the missing execution material became an
issue between them (§10).

Coras worked fast: most of the first draft was finished by **1 October 1560**,
three weeks after the *arrest*.

Davis's description of the structure is worth keeping in mind: the book is "both
a legal narrative and a literary interplay between a TEXT and ANNOTATIONS
(Coras's terms)," and it "tells two stories at once." Whatever one makes of that
as history, it describes the layout accurately, and it is an argument for
translating the annotations as carefully as the narrative.

Coras's printer's device and the book's last words are **`A RAISON CEDE`** — "yield
to reason." It is an anagram of *IEAN DE CORAS*. Ringold and Lewis declined to
translate it and left it standing in French; we should do the same, with a note.

A later printer's preface (1618) calls the book "presque une Tragi-comedie": the
*Protase* joyous and entertaining, full of the tricks of a false husband; the
*Epitase* uncertain and doubtful; the *Catastrophe* sad, pitiful and wretched.
Not Coras's own language, but a fair record of how his first readers took it.

**Why it matters.** Montaigne saw the trial as a boy and read the book. In
*Essais* III.11, *Des boyteux*:

> Je vy en mon enfance, un procez que Corras Conseiller de Thoulouse fit
> imprimer, d'un accident estrange; de deux hommes, qui se presentoient l'un pour
> l'autre…

and, on the court's confidence in condemning:

> Recevons quelque forme d'arrest qui die: La Cour n'y entend rien; Plus
> librement et ingenuement, que ne firent les Areopagites…

("Let us accept some form of decision that says: *The Court understands nothing of
it.*") Montaigne's use of the case is the single most-cited afterlife of the book
and belongs in any apparatus.

One caution. Montaigne's *je vy en mon enfance un procez que Corras … fit
imprimer* is normally read as "I saw in my childhood a trial *[report]* that
Coras had printed" — that is, he read the book. Some popular accounts, including
the English Wikipedia article on Martin Guerre, state that Montaigne **attended**
the sentencing on 12 September 1560. That cannot be squared with "en mon
enfance": Montaigne was born in 1533 and was twenty-seven in 1560. Treat the
attendance claim as **[unverified]** and do not repeat it.

In the twentieth century the case reached a wide
audience through Janet Lewis's novel *The Wife of Martin Guerre* (1941), Natalie
Zemon Davis's *The Return of Martin Guerre* (1983) and the film she advised on
(*Le Retour de Martin Guerre*, 1982), and the Robert Finlay / Davis controversy in
the *American Historical Review* in 1988 (§2, §10).

**The other contemporary account.** Guillaume Le Sueur, a law clerk to another
judge in the case, published a Latin pamphlet independently of Coras:
*Admiranda historia de Pseudo Martino Tholosae* (1561). It is a check on Coras at
some points and differs from him at others. Finlay never used it, which Davis
holds against him.

## 2. Timeline

Dates in the *Texte* are as Coras gives them. See §8 on old-style dating before
you take any January, February or March date at face value.

| Date | Event | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1527 | The Daguerre family leaves Hendaye, in the Basque Labourd, and settles at Artigat in the county of Foix (diocese of Rieux). The name is gallicised from *Daguerre* to *Guerre*. Martin is an infant — Coras's own text says Martin "was brought as a small child from the country of his birth, and at that time he was only approximately two years old," used by the court to explain why he spoke no Basque. | Year from Davis; the "about two years old" is Coras's |
| 1538 | Marriage of **Martin Guerre** and **Bertrande de Rols** at Artigat. Coras: Bertrande was "a young girl, nine or ten years old," and Martin "at that time also very young, and almost the same age." | Coras's own figures; the ages are probably conventional understatement and are disputed |
| 1538 – c. 1547 | The marriage is unconsummated: Bertrande deposes that "a little after her marriage … they lived eight or nine years together bound in an evil spell (*noué*) without being able to cohabit carnally," that her relatives urged her to seek a separation, and that she refused. The spell is broken by four masses and by eating consecrated wafers and *fouasses* (hearth cakes) given her by a priest. | Coras |
| c. 1547–48 | Birth of the son **Sanxi Guerre**. | Coras (immediately after the spell was lifted) |
| c. 1548 | Martin leaves Artigat "for a slight theft of wheat from his father." Coras says he stayed away eight years. | Year is an inference; contested |
| c. 1548 – 1556 | Martin in Spain, then Flanders. Coras's **annotations** (not the *Texte*) say: "ce Martin Guerre, qui s'en alla jeune garçon aux Espagnes, où le Cardinal de Burgos, & après son frere s'en servirent de laquais, & de là l'emmenerent en Flandres" — he served the **Cardinal of Burgos** and afterwards the Cardinal's brother as a lackey, and went with them to Flanders, where he bore arms against the French, Coras insists, under compulsion. | Coras, annotations |
| 10 August 1557 | Martin loses a leg to a cannon-shot before **Saint-Quentin**, "à la journée de S. Laurens" (St Lawrence's Day). He wears a wooden leg thereafter. Philip II is said to have granted him a lifetime place as a lay brother in a house of the Order of St John of Jerusalem. | Coras for the wound; the battle is independently dated; the Order of St John detail is from the secondary literature, **[unverified]** |
| summer 1556 | **Arnaud du Tilh**, called **Pansette**, of **Sajas**, arrives at Artigat as Martin Guerre and is accepted by Bertrande, by Martin's four sisters, by the uncle **Pierre Guerre** and by the village. At the first meeting he tells Bertrande to fetch "the white hose lined with white taffeta which I left in a certain chest when I went away" — and they are found there. | Year from Davis; Coras gives only "three years and more" |
| 1556–1559 | The impostor lives as Martin for "three years and more." Two daughters are born to him and Bertrande; one dies; the survivor is **Bernarde du Tilh**. He takes over Martin's property at Artigat and sells Guerre land at Hendaye. | Coras |
| c. 1552–53 | On his return from **Picardy**, du Tilh is taken for Martin Guerre by Dominique Pivol and Pierre de Guilhet — the origin of the whole scheme, as he confessed on the scaffold. | Coras, from the confession |
| c. 1558 | A soldier passing through Artigat "from Rochefort" denounces him aloud: Martin Guerre is in Flanders with a wooden leg. Three men — **Jean Espagnol**, an innkeeper; **Valentin Rougié**; **Pelegrin de Liberos** — recognise him privately; he silences them with a gesture and, in one case, with two handkerchiefs and a message for his brother Jean du Tilh. | Coras |
| c. 1558–59 | Quarrel with Pierre Guerre over the accounts of the estate during Martin's absence. The impostor sues; Pierre and his sons-in-law turn against him. Coras's text alleges a plot to kill him — an assault in Bertrande's presence, and an attempt to hire **Jean Loze**, consul of "Palhe," to pay for the killing. | Coras, reported as witness testimony |
| 1559 | A separate prosecution: **Jean d'Escornebœuf, called the Cadet**, sieur de Lanoux, has him arrested under the authority of the **Seneschal of Toulouse** — Coras does not state the charge in the *Texte*; Davis and the secondary literature give it as arson. He is released on appeal, returns to Artigat, and Bertrande receives him, washes his feet, and they go to bed. | Coras for the arrest and release; the arson charge is **[unverified]** against Coras |
| the next morning | Pierre Guerre, armed and with his sons-in-law, arrests him again — and, Coras notes pointedly, Pierre was not yet Bertrande's appointed guardian *ad litem*: the guardianship was not constituted until that evening after vespers, "as even this Pierre Guerre has since confessed." | Coras |
| "the month of January 1559" | **Bertrande de Rols** lodges her complaint before the **judge of Rieux**, demanding a double *amende honorable* and 2,000 *livres* with interest and costs. In new style this is very probably **January 1560** — see §8. | Date as Coras gives it; new-style equivalent is an inference |
| Jan – summer 1560 | The trial at Rieux. *Confrontemens*, a *monitoire* published at Le Pin, Sajas, Artigat and the neighbouring places, *enquestes d'office*. About **150 witnesses**: 30–40 swear he is Martin Guerre; a larger number swear he is Arnaud du Tilh; **60 and more** say the likeness is such that they dare not commit themselves. | Coras |
| 1560 | The judge of Rieux condemns du Tilh **to lose his head and be quartered**. He appeals to the **Parlement of Toulouse**. | Coras |
| 1560 | At Toulouse: **Jean de Coras** is *rapporteur*. The court summons Pierre Guerre and Bertrande in person and confronts them with the prisoner in full court. The prisoner holds up better than Bertrande, and "there were few judges present who were not persuaded that the prisoner was the true husband, and that the imposture came from the side of the wife and the uncle." | Coras — the court's leaning toward acquittal is Coras's own statement |
| 1560 | A supplementary *enqueste d'office*: of 25–30 new witnesses, 9 or 10 are sure he is Martin Guerre, 7 or 8 that he is Arnaud du Tilh, the rest in doubt. "*Mais quoy!*" — the judges were more uncertain than ever. | Coras |
| 1560, shortly before judgment | **The real Martin Guerre arrives from Spain with a wooden leg** and petitions to be heard. The court hears him under guard, then orders confrontations. Under questioning the newcomer answers *less* fluently than the prisoner; the court puts secret questions to each separately and both answer correctly, which makes the company suspect magic. | Coras |
| 1560 | The recognitions. The eldest sister looks at the newcomer, weeps, embraces him: "Here is my brother Martin Guerre." The other sisters and the leading witnesses follow. Bertrande, "trembling like a leaf shaken by the wind, her face all bathed in tears," runs to embrace him and begs his pardon. Martin refuses to be moved: "Put aside your tears, by which I cannot nor I ought not to be moved … for the disaster which has befallen our house no one is to blame but you." The court tried to excuse her and could not soften him. | Coras |
| 1560 | Reconciliation. The first president, **Mansencal**, is reported to have brought Martin and Bertrande together publicly, laying the blame on Martin for his desertion while telling Bertrande she was not free of fault. | Not in Coras's *Texte*; secondary literature, **[unverified]** |
| **12 September 1560** | The ***arrest***. The appeal is set aside; du Tilh is condemned for *imposture, faulseté, supposition de nom & personne, adultere, rapt, sacrilege, plage [plagiat], larrecin* — imposture, forgery, substitution of name and person, adultery, rape, sacrilege, enslavement and theft — to make *amende honorable* before the church of Artigat on his knees, in a shirt, bareheaded and barefoot, the halter round his neck and a burning wax taper in his hands; then to be led through the streets of Artigat to a gallows erected **before Martin Guerre's own house**, to be hanged and strangled, and his body afterwards burnt. His goods go to the daughter he fathered on Bertrande. Martin Guerre, Bertrande de Rols and Pierre Guerre are all discharged. | Coras, verbatim |
| **16 September 1560** | At Artigat the judge of Rieux hears du Tilh's full confession: he first conceived the fraud seven or eight years earlier, returning from **Picardy**, when **Dominique Pivol** and **Pierre de Guilhet** of Mane took him for Martin Guerre; he then gathered information systematically, and learned still more from Bertrande herself in bed. He denies necromancy. He names his debts, makes his daughter **Bernarde du Tilh** his heiress with **Jean du Tilh** (his brother, of Le Pin) and **Dominique Rebendaire** of Toulouse as her guardians and his executors. He repeats the confession three or four times, and again on the ladder, asking pardon of Martin Guerre, Bertrande and Pierre Guerre, "crying upon God for mercy, through His Son Jesus Christ." He is hanged, and his body burnt. | Coras, verbatim |

| after 1560 | Martin Guerre and Bertrande resumed life at Artigat and had further children. Bertrande is said to have died before the mid-1570s and Martin at some point before 1594. | Secondary literature only; **[unverified]** and loosely dated |

**Contested and uncertain items.** The ages at marriage; the exact year of
Martin's departure; whether the impotence lasted eight or nine years; whether
Pierre Guerre forged or merely procured Bertrande's complaint; the arson charge
behind the Escornebœuf prosecution; and above all **whether Bertrande de Rols
knew**. Coras's narrative is built on her having been deceived — that is what the
*arrest* requires, since she is discharged. Davis argued she must have known and
consented for much of the three years. See §10.

## 3. People

Spellings in the left column are Coras's own (verified against the French text).
**Keep the French forms.** Do not anglicise: *Jean*, not "John"; *Pierre*, not
"Peter"; *Bertrande*, not "Bertranda."

| Coras's form | Role | Render as |
|---|---|---|
| **Martin Guerre** | The husband. Born in the Basque Labourd c. 1524–25, brought to Artigat as an infant; absent c. 1548–1560; soldier in Spanish service; loses a leg at Saint-Quentin, 1557; returns to Toulouse during the appeal. | Martin Guerre |
| **Bertrande de Rols** | His wife. Married c. 1538; nominal plaintiff (*partie civile*) at Rieux; discharged by the *arrest*. Coras writes *de Rols* throughout, and often just *ladite de Rols*. | Bertrande de Rols; "the said de Rols" where Coras has *ladite de Rols* |

**Jean de Coras (1515–1572).** Born at Réalmont in the Albigeois, son of a
notary; the BnF and IdRef give **3 December 1515**, and some older references
1513 — give the date as "1515 (some sources 1513)". Studied law at Toulouse,
Cahors, Orléans and elsewhere; doctorate at Padua in 1535 according to the secondary literature — but Coras himself, in Annotation XXXV (p056), says Philippus Decius conferred his doctorate at **Siena in 1536**; prefer the primary statement and note the discrepancy. Taught at Toulouse
from 1536, then Valence (1545) and Ferrara (1550), where he was among the
best-known professors of his day; a legal humanist of the school of Alciato.
**Conseiller at the Parlement of Toulouse from 1552** — the office he holds on
the title page. Converted to Calvinism around 1558–1562, took part in the
Protestant rising at Toulouse in 1562, was excommunicated in 1565, and from about
1567 served **Jeanne d'Albret, Queen of Navarre, as chancellor**, and was at La
Rochelle in 1568–69. He was condemned to death in his absence in 1568 for serving
the Prince of Condé. On **4 October 1572**, six weeks after the Paris massacre of
St Bartholomew, armed Catholics broke into the prison of the palace of the
Parlement at Toulouse and killed some three hundred Protestant prisoners; Coras
was killed in his cell and his body hanged from the great elm in the Parlement's
courtyard, in his counsellor's robes. He died in the same *Conciergerie* in which
Arnaud du Tilh had been held — worth a note in the introduction, and worth
remembering when reading his annotations on hanging and on the mutilation of
bodies. Other works: commentaries on the Pandects, *De iuris arte libellus*
(1560), *Miscellaneorum iuris civilis libri*, the *Paraphrase sur l'Edict des
mariages clandestinement contractez*, and the posthumous *Centuria memorabilium
Curiae Tholosanae* (1599).

Our edition is dated **1572** — the year Coras was killed. Whether he saw it
through the press is **[unverified]**.

## 4. Places

| Place | What it is | Notes for translators |
|---|---|---|
| **Artigat** | The village, in the **Lèze valley**; today in the Ariège. Coras always calls it "Artigat **in the diocese of Rieux**," never "in the county of Foix" — and he is being precise. Artigat was a **Languedoc enclave** under the civil jurisdiction of the **seneschalsy of Toulouse**, surrounded by parishes (Lanoux, Castéras, Pailhès) that were in the county of Foix proper. Accounts that place Artigat "in the county of Foix" are geographically right and administratively wrong. Occitan-speaking. The Guerre house stood here, and the gallows was built in front of it. | Keep *Artigat*; follow Coras and say "in the diocese of Rieux." |
| **Le Pin** (Coras: *du Pin*) | **Le Pin-Murelet**, Haute-Garonne — about 3 km from Sajas, in the Savès, i.e. **in du Tilh country, not near Artigat**. Home of Jean du Tilh; the du Tilh patrimony lay here. Coras names it first in the list of places where the *monitoire* was published ("du Pin, Sagias, Artigat"), which is why it is easy to mistake for a neighbour of Artigat. It is not. | "Le Pin", or "Pin" following Coras's *du Pin*. |

**Administrative structure.** Artigat was a parish of the **diocese of Rieux**
and, for secular purposes, an enclave of Languedoc under the **seneschalsy of
Toulouse**. Criminal justice at first instance was exercised by the royal judge
at Rieux, from whom appeal lay to the **Parlement of Toulouse**. The separate
Escornebœuf prosecution went before the **Seneschal of Toulouse** — a different
first-instance court, which is why Coras's text has the prisoner arrested twice
by two different authorities. Sajas and Le Pin lay in the **Comminges**, a
different diocese: the impostor and the man he impersonated came from different
jurisdictions and different speech-communities, and the *monitoire* had to be
published in both.

**Languages.** Artigat spoke **Occitan**; Sajas spoke **Gascon**; Hendaye spoke
**Basque**. Coras makes the point himself — Basque is "a language quite different
from French and Gascon, little understandable except to those who are of the
country" — and the court disposed of it by finding that Martin, taken from
Hendaye at about two, had no Basque either. Davis notes that no witness was ever
asked about the prisoner's **voice or accent**: a striking silence, given that a
Gascon from Sajas was passing in an Occitan village.

**Distances** (straight-line, approximate — road distances in this country run
25–40% longer): Artigat to Rieux ~24 km; Artigat to Toulouse ~55 km; Sajas to
Toulouse ~42 km; Le Pin to Sajas ~3 km; Pailhès to Artigat ~4 km; Lanoux to
Artigat ~2 km; Artigat to Hendaye ~250 km; Artigat to Saint-Quentin ~760 km;
Artigat to Burgos ~430 km. These are computed from modern coordinates, not from
any sixteenth-century itinerary. In Coras's own measure a *lieue* ran to roughly
4 km here, but the unit was local and unstandardised — do not convert a *lieue*
in the text into kilometres without a note.

## 5. Procedure glossary

**The governing statute in 1560 is the Ordonnance de Villers-Cotterêts (August
1539)**, not the *Ordonnance criminelle* of 1670. The 1670 code is a legitimate
source for definitions but must never be presented as the law of this trial.
Three of its articles shape everything in Coras's *Texte*:

- **Art. 162** — the accused has **no counsel** and "shall answer with his own
  mouth"; parties and witnesses are heard "*séparément, secrètement et à part*."
  This is why the whole case turns on what people said in separate rooms, and why
  the convergence of Bertrande's and the prisoner's answers under separate
  examination is such a loaded fact (§10).
- **Art. 163** — a sentence of torture may be appealed, and criminal appeals go
  to the sovereign court "*immédiatement, et sans moyen*." This is the route by
  which du Tilh's case reached Toulouse.
- **Art. 149** — witnesses must be brought for *récolement* and *confrontation*,
  and the judge asks the accused for his *reproches* against a witness **before**
  the deposition is disclosed to him.

Arts. 110–111 also require the *arrêt* to be written unambiguously and **in
French** — which is why Coras can quote it as he does.

House rule on the two key words:

- ***arrest* / *arrêt*** → **"decision."** This follows Ringold and Lewis's title.
  Davis renders it "decree"; others use "final verdict" or keep the French
  italicised. All are defensible; we are picking one and holding to it. Never
  "arrest," never "judgment."
- ***sentence*** (of the judge of Rieux, or any lower judge) → **"judgment."**
  Keeping the two levels visibly distinct in English is the hinge of the book.

**How the appeal worked.** The judge of Rieux gave sentence; du Tilh appealed; by
the 1539 ordinance the appeal went straight to the Parlement without intermediate
steps. The dossier went to a *rapporteur* — Coras — who reported it to the
criminal chamber, which could confirm, quash, order further inquiry, or
substitute its own disposition, as it did. The proceeding was in writing and in
secret until sentence was pronounced. Nothing in Coras is a courtroom scene in
the Anglo-American sense, and the translation should not make it sound like one.

## 6. Legal citation conventions

### 6.1 Reading a civil-law citation

Sixteenth-century jurists did not cite the *Corpus iuris civilis* by numbers. They
cited **the opening word of the individual law, plus the rubric of the title it
stands under**, and left the reader to find it in his own folio. The order of the
two elements is free; `l. <incipit> ff. de <title>` is the commoner form in
French texts. Take `l. minorem D. de ritu nupt.`:

- `l.` = *lex* — an individual fragment within a title.
- `minorem` = the fragment's **incipit**, its opening word.
- `D.` (or `ff.`) = the **Digest**.
- `de ritu nupt.` = the title *De ritu nuptiarum*, "On the rite of marriage."

*De ritu nuptiarum* is D. 23.2; the fragment beginning *Minorem annis duodecim
nuptam…* is fr. 4 (Pomponius). The citation resolves to **D. 23.2.4**:

> Digest 23.2.4 (*De ritu nuptiarum*, l. *Minorem*)

Now take `l. si quis ff. de reg. iur.` The title is *De diversis regulis iuris
antiqui*, D. 50.17 — but it has 211 fragments and none beginning *Si quis* could
be confirmed. That is the normal case, and the reason for the rule in §6.4:
**cite the title, name the incipit, say the fragment is unidentified.** Do not
manufacture a number.

Refinements you will meet:

- `§` + a word = a paragraph within the fragment, itself named by its own incipit.
  `§ fin.` = the last paragraph.
- `in princ.` / `pr.` = *in principio*, the unnumbered opening block of a fragment
  before its first `§`. `in fin.` = *in fine*, at the end of the passage cited.
- `l. fin.` = the last law in the title; `l. un.` = the sole law in the title.
- `eo.` / `eod.` = *eodem titulo*, "in the same title"; `d. l.` = *dicta lege*,
  "the law already cited."
- Doctrine is cited **through** the text it glosses, never on its own:
  `Bart. in l. <incipit> ff. de <title>`; `gl. <first word of the gloss> in l. …`.

**On `ff.`** — conventionally explained either as a deformed Greek Π for
*Pandectae* or as a crossed **D**. Smith's *Dictionary* and the Ames Foundation
between them give both; give both rather than picking one.

**Volume divisions.** The pre-humanist *Corpus* was bound in five volumes, and a
citation sometimes names the volume rather than the book: *Digestum vetus*
(D. 1–24.2), *Infortiatum* (D. 24.3–38.17), *Digestum novum* (D. 39–50), *Codex*
(C. 1–9), *Volumen parvum* (C. 10–12, the *Authentica*, the Institutes).

### 6.2 The books and their sigla

| `de cons. dist. <n>` | Decretum, Part III (*De consecratione*) | modern `De cons. D. 2 c. 82` |
| `d.a.c.` / `d.p.c.` | Gratian's own linking passages (*dictum ante/post canonem*) | |
| `c. … X de <title>`, `extra de <title>` | **Liber Extra**, the Decretals of Gregory IX | `c. Odoardus, X, De solutionibus, III, 23` → modern `X 3.23.3` |
| `in VI`, `de reg. iur. in 6.` | **Liber Sextus** (Boniface VIII) | modern `VI 5.13.29` |
| `Clem.` | **Clementines** | modern `Clem. 3.14.2` |
| `Extrav.`, `Extrav. comm.` | **Extravagantes** (of John XXII; *communes*) | |
| `gl.`, `glo.` | the **Glossa ordinaria** — Accursius on the civil law | `gl. <incipit of the gloss> in l. …` |

**Three traps a translator will fall into.**

1. **`X` means *extra*, not the Roman numeral ten.** The Decretals are the
   collection "outside" Gratian.
2. **In the Decretum, `D.` = *distinctio* and `C.` = *causa*** — the exact
   opposite of their civil-law senses. The giveaway is a following `q.`
   (*quaestio*), or the absence of a title rubric.
3. **Several rubrics exist in both the Digest and the Code** — *de probationibus*,
   *de testibus*, *de poenis*, *de quaestionibus*, *ad legem Corneliam de falsis*.
   The `ff.` / `C.` marker is the only thing that disambiguates them, so never
   drop it. Likewise, a bare `de reg. iur.` is D. 50.17, but `de reg. iur. in VI`
   is the closing title of the Sext with its 88 *regulae*.

### 6.4 The house rendering pattern

In the text, **leave Coras's marginalia as he prints them**, abbreviations and
all, superscript letters preserved so each note stays matched to its sentence.
Put the expansion in a footnote:

> Digest 23.2.4 (*De ritu nuptiarum*, l. *Minorem*)

Modern locus first, the title in italics, then the *lex* by its incipit —
**italic and capitalised**, so it reads as a word of the text and not as a Latin
phrase meaning "the minor." When the fragment cannot be identified, drop the
numbers:

> Digest 50.17 (*De diversis regulis iuris antiqui*); the fragment beginning
> *Si quis* has not been identified.

Use "cf." rather than "=" when inferring: generic incipits — *Si quis*, *Cum*,
*Item*, *Nemo*, *Qui* — open dozens of fragments, and early printings differ.
**Never supply a numeric locus you have not verified.** An unlocated citation
reported honestly beats a plausible fabrication.

Two precedents. The St Andrews CLICME edition of a medieval *Summula* inserts the
modern citation in square brackets **in the text**, on the ground that the
citations are argument, not apparatus. Scott and Pearl's English Bodin states
plainly that it drops the marginal citations altogether. We do neither: we keep
them and footnote them, and we say so in the front matter.

## 8. Money, measures, calendar, address

**Money.** The *livre tournois* was the unit of account: **1 livre = 20 sous
(sols) = 240 deniers.** An ordinance of 1549 made the *livre tournois* compulsory
for contracts, so it is the unit in which the court reckons. The **écu** was a
gold coin in actual circulation whose value in *livres* fluctuated across the
century (roughly three to six *livres* over the period); the *livre tournois* was
displaced as the official unit of account by the *écu* only in 1577. Render
*livres*, *sous* and *deniers* untranslated and italicised, with a note on first
use. Do not convert to pounds, and do not attempt a modern equivalent.

Figures in the text: Bertrande claims **2,000 livres** with interest and costs;
the estate in dispute between the impostor and Pierre Guerre is put at **seven to
eight thousand livres**.

**Measures.** The *lieue* (league) varied by region; in the south-west it ran
around 4 km, but there was no standard. Keep *lieue* → "league" and note the
imprecision rather than converting. Shoe sizes in the text are given in *points*
(Martin was fitted at twelve, the prisoner at nine) — keep "points."

**Calendar — this is the one that will bite.** In France before the **Edict of
Roussillon (9 August 1564, article 39)**, the legal year began at **Easter**, not
1 January. The edict moved the year's beginning to 1 January and was applied from
**1 January 1567**. Everything in the Martin Guerre affair therefore predates the
reform.

Consequence: **"In the month of January 1559," the opening words of Coras's
*Texte*, almost certainly means January 1560 by modern reckoning.** Easter 1559
fell on 26 March; the following Easter on 14 April 1560. A January falling
between them is styled 1559 in the French old style and 1560 new style. This also
fits the chronology: a complaint in January 1560, a trial at Rieux in the spring
and summer, an appeal, and the *arrest* on 12 September 1560.

House rule: **keep Coras's date as printed, and add the new-style equivalent in
square brackets on first occurrence** — "In the month of January 1559 [= 1560
n.s.]" — with a front-matter note. Do the same for any February or March date.
Dates from 25 March or Easter onward need no adjustment. September 1560 is
September 1560 either way.

## 10. Contested points and the Davis–Finlay exchange

Coras's *Texte* is the only substantial record of the trial, and it is not a
neutral one. He is a judge explaining why his court nearly acquitted an impostor,
and the *arrest* he is defending discharges Bertrande de Rols. His narrative has
a structural interest in her innocence. On his own account she was "easily
deceived by the cunning and craftiness of men," "given the weakness of her sex,"
and her motive was *l'incroyable envie, qu'icelle de Rolz avoit, de recouvrer son
mary* — the incredible longing to have her husband back.

At the same time Coras plainly admires his impostor. He calls the affair a
tragedy for "this fine peasant," and even Finlay, Davis's sharpest critic,
concedes that Coras "unequivocally condemned Arnaud, yet … had a certain
admiration for the impostor's abilities." That double note must survive
translation.

**The quarrel.** *AHR Forum*, *American Historical Review* 93, no. 3 (June 1988):
Robert Finlay, "The Refashioning of Martin Guerre," 553–571; Natalie Zemon Davis,
"On the Lame," 572–603. Davis's title alludes to Montaigne's *Des boyteux*.

| Question | Finlay | Davis |
|---|---|---|
| Did Bertrande know? | No. She was "universally regarded as the impostor's victim, not his accomplice"; the question "never arose at the time"; among all the witnesses "apparently no one ever suggested that Bertrande was Arnaud's accomplice. No one, that is, until Davis." | She was deceived at first, then became a collaborator — "though toward the end not always a wholehearted one." The village response was "a mix of being deceived and being deceptive or acquiescent." |
| On what evidence? | On none that is new: the thesis "depends on her mere assertion that she has recognized a truth that apparently remained hidden from both the villagers of Artigat and the judges of Toulouse." | On four arguments, not one: (1) the impostor's impossible knowledge of people he had never met, which Coras himself could not explain by coaching and half-attributed to magic; (2) the gross difference in **body shape** — Martin tall, thin and dark, the prisoner short, stocky and thick-legged, plus the shoemaker's lasts; (3) Bertrande's conduct between the accusation and the complaint, above all that she washed the prisoner's feet and slept with him one night and agreed to be plaintiff the next day, *sans nouvelles preuves* — Coras's own phrase; (4) the convergence, under **separate** interrogation, of her intimate testimony with his. |
| The sexual argument | Demolishes it by arithmetic: after nine years of impotence, a pregnancy, and eight years of absence, "Martin and Bertrande could only have had sexual intercourse for a few months at most." | Concedes it — "there is sexual learning; here, Robert Finlay and I agree" — and rebases on body shape: she "was feeling a body quite unlike the one she had lain next to … unclothed as well as clothed." |
| Why then did she prosecute? | If they were accomplices, all she had to do was refuse to be plaintiff; Davis never explains why that "obvious and safe course" was not taken. | Pierre and her mother threatened to turn her out; her children, the family property, her reputation and possibly her life were at stake; and she had her own doubts. On the same day she sent the prisoner clothing and money. |
| Is Coras trustworthy? | He is the best source there is, and Davis reinterprets him against his plain meaning; the *Arrest Memorable* "is a document that Davis implies is not to be trusted … precisely because it fails to yield up the characters called for by her reinterpretation." | The book "tells two stories at once." It has conclusions, "but the reader is left with the reasoning and evidence from which to construct alternate views." She also notes Finlay used only the 1561 edition and never consulted Le Sueur. |
| Did Coras identify with the impostor? | Concedes admiration, rejects identification: "Nothing is cited from Coras's text to support these contentions." Coras expressly warned that he meant no equation of "such an impudent impostor with such noble, grand, and illustrious persons." | Urges parallels Coras could have felt: rhetorical skill, rebelliousness, and — from Aldo Stella's work on Coras's student years — a Paduan circle accused of owning "magical and illicit books," against an Arnaud suspected of magic in his youth. |
| Protestantism | No warrant in the sources: the Artigat altar was smashed eight years after the execution, the de Rols Protestantism is documented only in the mid-seventeenth century, and Arnaud's blaspheming youth "does not bespeak receptivity to the Reformed religion." | The converted blasphemer is "a topos of Reformed martyrologies" like Crespin's. She documents converts and pastors around Artigat in the late 1550s, and notes that Arnaud died crying "mercy to God by his son Jesus Christ" with **no invocation of Mary or the saints** — which two Protestant authors would both have registered. She insists it "remains a mere possibility throughout my text, never a certainty," and corrects Finlay's "non-sacramental view of marriage": clandestine marriage before *Tametsi* (1563) was disapproved but sacramental and indissoluble. |
| "Self-fashioning" | A concept from Greenblatt's work on **elite** literature, with nothing in the record to support its application to peasants. He also counts Davis's names for the impostor: "Pansette" six times, "Arnaud" ten, "the new Martin" forty-five. | The term "derives from Montaigne" — *On s'y forme, on s'y façonne … car la dissimulation est des plus notables qualitez de ce siècle*. |

**Where they agree**, which is worth a footnote of its own: both accept that
Bertrande was genuinely taken in at the first encounter, and that the sisters
were taken in first and welcomed him first. The dispute is over whether the
deception went on being one.

**Why this matters for the translation.** Davis's reading rests substantially on
Coras's **Annotations 97 and 98**, where he regroups the arguments for and against
Bertrande's guilt and admits that "the greatest difficulties in judging this trial
… were whether Martin Guerre and Bertrande de Rols should be condemned," the
difficulty as to Bertrande being "even greater." **Those annotations are not in
Ringold and Lewis.** The material the central modern controversy turns on is
precisely the material never put into English. That is the argument for this
project, and it belongs in the introduction.

Two consequences. Translate **literally**: do not smooth Coras's hesitations,
repetitions, self-contradictions or his admiration for the prisoner, since those
are the features the argument runs on, and do not flatten *prodigieux*,
*tragédie* and *tragi-comédie*, which are hot words in 1560. And do not adopt
either position as settled: where Coras asserts Bertrande's innocence, translate
the assertion; where he betrays doubt, translate the doubt; annotate the
dispute.

**Other points on which the evidence is thin**, and which should carry a note
rather than a confident statement: the ages at marriage (Coras's "nine or ten
years old" is below the canonical minimum and may be rhetorical); the year Martin
left; whether Bertrande or Pierre Guerre really initiated the complaint (Coras
files it in her name, records the defence's allegation that Pierre "induced and
suborned" her and the evidence that the suit was brought against her will, and
never resolves the conflict); the identity of the soldier from Rochefort, never
heard in court; the charge in the Escornebœuf prosecution; the name of the judge
of Rieux; and whether the court would in fact have acquitted — Coras says the
judges were nearly persuaded, and he is both the source and an interested party.

**A loose end in the record.** Davis notes that a "Martin de Guerre" was executed
at Rouen in 1555 for carrying enemy letters from Burgos through Irun and Bayonne
to Spanish merchants. She raises the coincidence and dismisses it. It is worth a
footnote and nothing more.

**A discrepancy in the numbers.** Coras's *Texte* gives "one hundred and fifty
witnesses heard" at Rieux and "twenty-five or thirty" in the supplementary
inquiry at Toulouse. Finlay writes of "180 witnesses" taken by the court, which
appears to be the sum of the two rather than an independent figure. Keep Coras's
numbers in the translation and note the aggregate in the apparatus.
